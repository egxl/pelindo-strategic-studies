# **Strategic Ecosystem Analysis of the Port of Tanjung Priok: Stakeholder Dynamics, Cargo Journey Architecture, Systemic Coordination Failures, and Diagnostic Protocols**

## **Institutional and Commercial Architecture of the Port Ecosystem**

The Port of Tanjung Priok serves as Indonesia’s primary maritime trade gateway, processing over 50% of the country’s international container traffic and transshipment cargo1. With container volumes reaching 7.6 million Twenty-Foot Equivalent Units (TEUs) in 2024 and expanding past 8 million TEUs, the port anchors the national economy and drives the maritime trade corridors of West Java1. Following the national consolidation of state-owned port operators under PT Pelabuhan Indonesia (Pelindo), port assets are managed regionally by Pelindo Regional 24.  
Despite administrative consolidation, the operational reality of Tanjung Priok remains fragmented across public border authorities, specialized terminal operating companies, transport intermediaries, labor collectives, and adjacent urban settlements. The port functions as a socio-technical system where administrative mandates and private commercial incentives intersect, often producing institutional friction.

### **Commercial Waterfront and Terminal Operators**

International ocean carriers and domestic coastal lines operate under conflicting commercial pressures. Global container lines, represented by international carriers such as Maersk, MSC, CMA CGM, COSCO, and Evergreen, prioritize berth-on-arrival status, high crane productivity (targeting over 25 to 30 moves per gross crane hour), and rapid vessel turnaround times to preserve scheduled liner loops3. Domestic shipping companies, operating under the cabotage regime through the Indonesian National Shipowners' Association (INSA), rely on Tanjung Priok as the primary consolidation hub for the national Sea Tollway (Tol Laut) program, balancing smaller vessel capacities with slower domestic cargo clearing cycles4.  
Both groups face operational bottlenecks, including draft limitations along older terminal quays, pilotage and tug arrival delays, and high empty-equipment repositioning costs caused by regional trade imbalances5. Ocean carriers exert substantial commercial influence through vessel routing choices and the ability to apply congestion surcharges5. However, they remain dependent on Pelindo Marine for harbor navigation services and on terminal concessionaires for quay crane deployment8. This structure creates commercial tension with cargo owners over container detention and demurrage charges during customs holds, as well as with terminal operators over handling charges and disputed berthing priorities5.  
Terminal operations are divided among specialized container terminals: the Jakarta International Container Terminal (JICT), TPK Koja, New Priok Container Terminal 1 (NPCT1), and IPC Terminal Petikemas (IPC TPK)10. Terminal concessionaires balance quay crane utilization with yard occupancy ratios (YOR), aiming to maintain yard density between 60% and 65% to prevent yard locking and uncoordinated container shifts10. These operators face chronic yard congestion driven by uncleared import cargo, sudden regulatory shifts that freeze customs clearance, and gate congestion caused by uncoordinated truck arrivals10.  
Terminal operators hold substantial operational gatekeeping authority over customs-bonded areas (Lini 1), giving them control over yard handling fees, progressive dwell-time storage rates, and inter-terminal transfers (overbrengen)10. However, they depend directly on Customs (Bea Cukai) for electronic release documents, on stevedoring unions for dockside labor, and on standardized operating platforms such as TOS Nusantara, which integrates the PARAMA and PRAYA terminal execution systems9. This environment generates operational friction with beneficial cargo owners, who often use terminal container yards as low-cost storage space, and with drayage operators over terminal turn-around times (TRT) and equipment availability3.

### **Intermediaries and Cargo Consignees**

Beneficial Cargo Owners (BCOs), spanning multinational automotive and electronics manufacturers in West Java's industrial belts (Cikarang, Karawang, and Bekasi) down to domestic commercial importers and exporters, require end-to-end supply chain reliability, low landed logistics costs, and rapid cargo turnaround19. Cargo owners face unpredictable non-tariff trade measures, unexpected red-channel physical inspections, terminal storage surcharges, and container detention billing9.  
While large industrial conglomerates possess sufficient political and economic leverage to secure priority customs clearance (such as MITA or Authorized Economic Operator status), medium and small enterprises remain vulnerable to administrative delays10. Cargo owners rely on freight forwarders for document processing, drayage fleets for inland carriage, and technical ministries for import permits22. This fragmentation incentivizes opportunism: when commercial warehouse fees outside the port exceed terminal storage tariffs, cargo owners frequently leave containers in terminal yards, treating working marine terminals as temporary storage facilities9.  
Freight forwarders and customs brokerage firms (Perusahaan Pengurusan Jasa Kepabeanan, PPJK), organized under the Indonesian Logistics and Forwarders Association (ALFI/ILFA), manage the administrative and physical handoffs between shippers, shipping lines, border regulators, and terminal gates22. These intermediaries require rapid document processing, clear tariff classifications, automated electronic Delivery Order (e-DO) releases, and predictable physical inspection schedules22.  
They face system downtimes across ministerial single-window portals, overlapping inspection demands from Customs and Quarantine, and off-dock empty container depot delays26. Forwarders exert operational control over routing choices and inland transport selection, but lack the authority to resolve ministerial regulatory impasses. They remain dependent on technical trade data from importers, shipping line approvals, and access to national digital portals like CEISA and INSW9. Forwarders regularly clash with container lines over slow container deposit returns and with terminal customer service offices over disputed handling charges.

### **Landside Transport and Drayage Fleets**

Container drayage and road transport, coordinated by the Indonesian Trucking Association (Asosiasi Pengusaha Truk Indonesia, APTRINDO), moves more than 90% of the port's containerized freight, dispatching between 6,000 and 17,000 truck movements per day14. Trucking operators seek to maximize truck asset turnover by completing multiple round trips per vehicle each day, reducing gate turn-around times below 45 minutes, and minimizing empty runs3.  
Drayage fleets face severe congestion along port approach corridors, long terminal gate queues, slow container handling at off-dock empty depots, and aging tractor units, with over 90% of the local fleet challenged by modern urban emissions standards14. Drayage operators exert disruption leverage; wildcat strikes or organized transport stoppages can stall port operations and back up traffic throughout North Jakarta.  
Trucking companies depend on terminal automated gate systems, the Single Truck Identification Data (STID) framework, the Terminal Booking System (TBS), and depot container handling equipment5. Consequently, structural conflict persists between trucking firms and terminal operators over gate system outages, long vehicle queues, and police enforcement targeting Over Dimension Over Load (ODOL) vehicles and municipal route curfews29.

### **Sovereign Border Governance and Regulatory Authorities**

Border management and regulatory enforcement involve three primary statutory entities: the Directorate General of Customs and Excise (DJBC), the Indonesian Quarantine Authority (Badan Karantina Indonesia, Barantin), and the Tanjung Priok Port Authority (Kantor Otoritas Pelabuhan Utama / KSOP)13.  
Customs operates through the KPU Bea dan Cukai Tipe A Tanjung Priok under the Ministry of Finance, balancing revenue collection targets, smuggling enforcement, trade facilitation, and dwelling time targets under the National Logistics Ecosystem (NLE)13. Customs officers face fraudulent import declarations, technical valuation disputes, and shifting trade regulations (Lartas) issued by trade and industrial ministries21.  
Customs holds substantial statutory power: officers can legally inspect, delay, seize, or re-export cargo13. The agency relies on shipping line manifest filings (BC 1.1), line-ministry approvals within the Indonesia National Single Window (INSW), and integrated physical inspection facilities (Tempat Pemeriksaan Fisik Terpadu, TPFT)26. Tension emerges between Customs' enforcement mandate and the commercial pressure from logistics operators to accelerate clearance velocity34.  
The Indonesian Quarantine Authority (Barantin) manages national biosecurity, preventing the introduction of agricultural pests, animal diseases, and marine pathogens19. Barantin inspectors face limited biosecure inspection facilities, slow physical container handling by terminal operators, and missing sanitary certificates from country-of-origin ports37.  
Barantin holds absolute statutory authority to block cargo discharge, mandate fumigation, order re-exports, or destroy non-compliant shipments19. The agency operates through the digital Single Submission Quarantine Customs (SSm QC) platform, sharing physical examination sheds at TPFT Graha Segara and CDC Banda with Customs19. Conflicts arise with cargo owners over testing laboratory wait times, and with terminal operators over long yard dwell times caused by pending biosecurity decisions37.  
The Tanjung Priok Port Authority (KSOP), operating under the Directorate General of Sea Transportation of the Ministry of Transportation, serves as the sovereign landlord regulator, overseeing navigation safety, terminal concessions, environmental compliance, and statutory dwelling time targets8. KSOP balances public logistics efficiency against the commercial strategies of private and state-owned terminal operators8.  
The Port Authority issues harbor master vessel departure approvals (Surat Persetujuan Berlayar, SPB) and manages port concession agreements, but relies on Pelindo for infrastructure data and maintenance8. KSOP coordinates inter-agency dispute resolution, often mediating between commercial trucking associations, municipal transport departments (Dishub DKI Jakarta), and labor unions29.

### **Labor Collectives and Host Municipalities**

Dockside stevedoring labor is organized under the Koperasi Tenaga Kerja Bongkar Muat (TKBM) Upaya Karya, an institutional cooperative holding exclusive longshore labor provisioning rights at Tanjung Priok16. The TKBM cooperative focuses on preserving union job rosters, securing negotiated wage levels, protecting collective bargaining rights, and resisting unilateral labor automation or open-market contracting16.  
Dockers work in hazardous environments and face variable daily shift earnings alongside long-term displacement risks from automated handling equipment42. TKBM maintains high disruption leverage: backed by the Tripartite Joint Decree of 2011 (SKB 2 Dirjen 1 Deputi), the cooperative holds a legal monopoly over port handling labor, giving it the capacity to halt dock operations through strikes39.  
TKBM depends on stevedoring firms (Perusahaan Bongkar Muat, PBM) and terminal operational shifts for labor allocations16. The cooperative faces ongoing scrutiny from government anti-corruption bodies (Stranas PK), stevedoring employer associations (APBMI), and modern terminal concessionaires, all of which seek to reform the 2011 decree to introduce competitive, performance-based contracting39.  
The residential communities of North Jakarta surrounding the port—including Koja, Cilincing, and Tanjung Priok—bear the environmental and social costs of port freight operations29. These communities prioritize road safety, lower exhaust emissions, reduced noise, and local employment access29.  
Local residents face road congestion, high traffic accident rates involving heavy drayage trucks, diesel particulate pollution, structural vibrations, and stormwater runoff problems exacerbated by port land reclamation29. While nearby communities hold limited direct commercial leverage, they exert political influence through regional parliaments (DPRD DKI) and neighborhood actions, which can lead to municipal trucking curfews, roadway zoning restrictions, and protests that interrupt port corridor access29.

## **End-to-End Cargo Journey Analysis**

Container movements through Tanjung Priok involve interconnected physical handling steps, digital validations, and regulatory checkpoints. Operational paths diverge substantially between import and export workflows, creating specific friction points at each stage of the cargo journey.

### **Import Cargo Journey Architecture**

The import container journey begins forty-eight to twenty-four hours before a vessel berths, when the ocean carrier transmits the electronic inward cargo manifest (BC 1.1) to Customs through the CEISA platform, while simultaneously filing vessel arrival notices with KSOP through Inaportnet. The beneficial cargo owner or their customs broker (PPJK) then submits the import declaration (Pemberitahuan Impor Barang, PIB) into the CEISA 4.0 system through the Indonesia National Single Window (INSW)26. Import duties, excise, and import taxes are calculated and settled electronically through state billing modules.  
For biosecure commodities, including agricultural products, live animals, fish, and wood products, a parallel declaration is submitted through the Single Submission Quarantine Customs (SSm QC) platform19. The Customs targeting engine, running alongside Barantin’s risk assessment system, processes the data and assigns the shipment to an administrative clearance channel:

* *Green Channel (Jalur Hijau):* Immediate electronic release without physical inspection or document review.  
* *Yellow Channel (Jalur Kuning):* Mandatory documentary audit to verify trade permits (Lartas), certificates of origin, or valuation declarations prior to cargo release.  
* *Red Channel (Jalur Merah):* Mandatory non-intrusive container scanning or physical cargo unstuffing (behandle) at an integrated inspection facility20.

Once the vessel receives berthing approval via Inaportnet, Pelindo Marine deploys harbor tugs and licensed pilots to navigate the ship to its assigned terminal quay at JICT, TPK Koja, NPCT1, or IPC TPK. Ship-to-Shore (STS) gantry cranes, operated alongside TKBM union dockers who release container twistlocks on board, discharge containers directly onto internal terminal haulage chassis16. Terminal tractors transfer the boxes to designated yard blocks, where Rubber-Tyred Gantry (RTG) cranes or Automated Stacking Cranes (ASC), directed by TOS Nusantara and the PARAMA terminal system, stack the containers according to yard optimization algorithms17.  
For cargo routed to the red channel or flagged for mandatory quarantine inspection, the container is moved from the yard stack and shunted to a dedicated Tempat Pemeriksaan Fisik Terpadu (TPFT) facility, such as TPFT Graha Segara or CDC Banda20. Under the SSm QC system, Customs officers and Barantin quarantine inspectors perform a single joint physical inspection19. Private handling teams unpack the container to the designated inspection depth (ranging from 10% to 100%), allowing officers to inspect the contents36. Alternatively, containers assigned to non-intrusive inspection pass through high-energy x-ray scanners (Hi-Co Scan) to verify internal contents against the electronic manifest without physical unstuffing35. Once biosecurity and customs requirements are met, Customs generates an electronic release approval (Surat Persetujuan Pengeluaran Barang, SPPB), which is transmitted to the importer, shipping line, and terminal operator9.  
The final landside stage begins when the cargo owner pays shipping line local handling charges to obtain an Electronic Delivery Order (e-DO), and settles terminal storage and lift-on/lift-off charges through Pelindo’s Single Billing system11. The transport company registers an available container chassis within the Single Truck Identification Data (STID) system and reserves a gate appointment slot using the Terminal Booking System (TBS)5.  
Upon arrival at the terminal, automated gate cameras scan the truck’s license plate, read the STID RFID transponder, verify the e-DO, and issue a digital Equipment Interchange Receipt (EIR) alongside a yard bay assignment ticket25. The drayage truck enters the yard, where an RTG crane loads the import container onto the chassis50. The vehicle then clears the exit gate, where weigh-in-motion sensors verify axle weights, before entering the arterial road network and proceeding via the Tanjung Priok Access (TJPA) toll road toward inland industrial plants29.

### **Export Cargo Journey Architecture**

The export workflow begins inland when an exporter secures a booking confirmation and equipment release order from an ocean carrier, authorizing the pickup of an empty container from an off-dock container depot. Drayage trucks haul the empty box to the exporter's facility for loading and secure cargo stuffing. Once packed, the container is weighed at a certified weighing station to generate a Verified Gross Mass (VGM) certificate, required by SOLAS regulations to prevent vessel overloading, which is then uploaded into the terminal operating portal25.  
The exporter files an export declaration (Pemberitahuan Ekspor Barang, PEB) through CEISA 4.0 via the INSW single window. Shipments containing agricultural, animal, or marine products are submitted through the export module of SSm QC to secure required sanitary or phytosanitary export certificates19. Following document validation, Customs issues an Export Service Note (Nota Pelayanan Ekspor, NPE), authorizing the cargo to enter the export terminal.  
The trucking dispatcher books a delivery slot through the Terminal Booking System (TBS) and pairs the container number with an STID-registered truck5. The vehicle arrives at the terminal entry gate, where optical character recognition systems and radiation portal monitors (RPM) verify container markings, driver identity, and radiological safety before printing the gate-in interchange ticket25.  
An RTG crane unloads the box in an export yard block, positioning it according to a vessel stowage plan that accounts for container weight class, size, discharge port sequence, and vessel stability constraints17. Once import discharging operations on the vessel conclude, internal terminal tractors move the pre-marshalled export containers to the quayside. Ship-to-Shore cranes hoist the boxes into ship cells, where TKBM longshoremen secure twistlocks and deck lashing gear16.  
The shipping line submits the final outward cargo manifest (BC 1.2) to Customs, and the Port Authority (KSOP) issues the vessel departure clearance (Surat Persetujuan Berlayar, SPB), allowing the ship to depart8.

## **Systemic Coordination Failures in Port Operations**

Operational bottlenecks at Tanjung Priok stem from institutional misalignments, uncoordinated operational handoffs, and split commercial incentives across public and private actors. The following five coordination failures generate sustained dwelling-time spikes, raise logistics costs, and limit throughput capacity.

### **The Regulatory Misalignment and Import Restriction Policy Paradox**

A recurring driver of container dwelling time spikes is the misalignment between trade regulations issued by the Ministry of Trade (Kemendag), technical industrial verifications (Pertimbangan Teknis, Pertek) managed by the Ministry of Industry (Kemenperin), and border enforcement executed by Customs21. The implementation of restrictive border controls—such as the import governance updates under Permendag 36/2023—shifted thousands of Harmonized System (HS) product lines from post-border compliance checks to active border enforcement21.  
Because line ministries lacked the administrative capacity to process required technical permits before inbound shipments arrived, containers accumulated rapidly across primary terminal yards21. This administrative backlog trapped over 26,000 containers across Indonesia's major ports, with more than 17,000 import containers stranded at Tanjung Priok alone21.  
This regulatory friction was exacerbated by commercial pricing structures. Terminal storage tariffs in customs-bonded zones (Lini 1\) were lower than commercial bonded warehouse rates outside the port or the chassis demurrage costs charged by private trucking companies9.  
Major importers—most visibly multinational automotive firms importing completely built-up units and knocked-down components—deliberately left uncleared containers inside terminal yards, treating working marine terminals as low-cost storage facilities9. This behavior pushed yard occupancy ratios at JICT, TPK Koja, and NPCT1 well past 80%, forcing terminal operators to perform excessive container shifts that slowed quay crane productivity and increased vessel turn-around times10.  
Resolving the resulting port congestion required urgent ministerial revisions under Permendag 8/2024 to remove the bottlenecked permit requirements, alongside executive directives ordering round-the-clock customs operations23. This episode demonstrated how unilateral regulatory changes made without coordinating with terminal operators can turn operational container yards into storage buffers, driving port dwell times from an average 2.47–2.88 days up to 3.5 days within weeks18.

### **The Digital-Physical Disconnect in Joint Border Inspections**

Under the National Logistics Ecosystem (NLE) framework, the rollout of Single Submission Quarantine Customs (SSm QC) aimed to streamline border processing by replacing separate agency filings with a single digital document submission and a single joint physical inspection26. The digital interface, managed through the Indonesia National Single Window (INSW), operates efficiently by coordinating inspection notifications across Customs and Barantin databases26. However, physical ground operations at dedicated examination facilities (such as TPFT Graha Segara and CDC Banda) remain vulnerable to operational delays35.  
These physical delays stem from fragmented operational handoffs:

> 1. *Shunting and Staging Delays:* Once a container is flagged for red-channel or biosecurity inspection, it must be moved from terminal storage stacks to an off-dock inspection yard20. This move depends on available terminal haulage chassis, inter-terminal transfers, and yard crane availability, often taking 24 to 36 hours merely to position the container36.  
> 2. *Desynchronized Inspector Shifts:* While the digital platform issues a single joint inspection notice, field inspectors from Customs and Barantin frequently work on different shift schedules. If one agency's inspector is delayed by other audits or sampling duties, the container sits uninspected on the open bay, exposed to weather risks19.  
> 3. *Secondary Handling Disputes:* Because container destuffing and handling labor inside the TPFT is contracted through specialized stevedoring pools, disputes frequently arise among cargo owners, forwarders, and operators over handling charges and damages incurred during destuffing36.

As a result of these physical bottlenecks, while customs clearance for standard green-channel cargo takes less than half a day (averaging 0.42 days)34, red-channel and quarantine cargo can sit for four to six days, keeping overall port dwell times higher than regional benchmark ports10.

### **Landside Appointment Saturation and Fleet Asynchrony**

To smooth truck arrival patterns and reduce landside gate congestion, Pelindo Regional 2 introduced the Single Truck Identification Data (STID) framework and deployed the Terminal Booking System (TBS) across its container terminals5. The system requires transport companies to book discrete gate arrival windows linked to valid electronic container delivery orders (e-DO)3.  
However, the booking system frequently breaks down due to operating conditions across the local trucking sector:

* *Fleet Obsolescence:* According to trucking industry data from APTRINDO, nearly 95% of the heavy container trucks operating around Tanjung Priok are older vehicles prone to mechanical breakdowns and lacking real-time GPS fleet tracking systems29.  
* *External Corridor Gridlock:* Chronic traffic jams along the main arterial access roads (such as Jl. Cakung-Cilincing, Jl. Jampea, and the Koja access roads) make it difficult for drivers to arrive within their scheduled TBS appointment windows8. A driver delayed by two hours in an external traffic jam forfeits their booked appointment window, forcing them to cancel, rebook, or wait outside the gates55.  
* *Shortage of Buffer Staging Areas:* Because the port has limited dedicated off-dock staging yards (buffer zones), drivers who miss their scheduled appointment windows park along the shoulders of public roads and residential avenues in North Jakarta3.

This dynamic creates a self-reinforcing congestion loop: local road congestion causes missed appointment slots, creating informal roadside staging queues that further block moving traffic along the port's arterial corridors. Consequently, terminal gates experience alternating periods of idle processing capacity followed by intense arrival spikes that push truck turnaround times (TRT) well past 60 to 90 minutes3.

### **The Intermodal Rail-Maritime Cost and Infrastructure Penalty**

Although Tanjung Priok features direct rail freight links (connecting the Jakarta Port Container Terminal rail line through Stasiun Pasoso and onward to dry ports like Cikarang Dry Port), rail intermodal freight accounts for less than 3% of the port's total container movements7. The overwhelming majority of containers continue to move exclusively via road drayage, despite severe highway traffic across the Jakarta–Cikampek industrial corridor48.  
This failure to establish a working rail intermodal corridor is rooted in structural cost and infrastructure barriers:

* *Double-Handling Cost Penalties:* Moving a container by rail from Cikarang to Tanjung Priok requires drayage from the industrial plant to the dry port, a lift-on/lift-off (Lo-Lo) fee onto the rail wagon, the state rail freight charge (PT Kereta Api Logistik), unloading at Pasoso station, and a secondary drayage haul through congested port roads into the actual marine terminal yard, adding another terminal Lo-Lo fee54. The resulting terminal handling and secondary transfer charges make rail shipping 20% to 35% more expensive per box than direct road trucking54.  
* *Shared Rail Infrastructure Constraints:* Freight trains must share track capacity with the heavily congested commuter rail lines (KRL Commuterline Jabodetabek) across the Jakarta railway network, severely limiting freight slot allocations during peak daylight hours61.  
* *Missing Railhead Integration:* Marine terminal container yards (JICT, TPK Koja, NPCT1) lack on-dock rail tracks alongside the container berths, preventing direct transfers between ship-to-shore cranes or yard gantries and rail wagons54. Every rail container must be moved through a secondary road transfer within the port perimeter, negating the efficiency and cost benefits of rail freight54.

### **Institutional Lock-In and Waterfront Labor Monopoly**

The waterfront labor model at Tanjung Priok remains governed by a legacy tripartite decree: the Joint Decree of Two Directors General and One Deputy (SKB 2 Dirjen 1 Deputi of 2011\) issued by the Ministry of Transportation, Ministry of Manpower, and the Ministry of Cooperatives and SMEs39. This framework grants an exclusive institutional monopoly over dockside labor provisioning to a single entity: Koperasi Tenaga Kerja Bongkar Muat (TKBM) Upaya Karya16.  
This institutional arrangement creates continuous operational and economic tension:

* *High-Cost Labor Structure:* The national corruption prevention strategy agency (Stranas PK) and stevedoring employer associations (APBMI) have repeatedly pointed out that this labor monopoly increases port handling costs through mandatory crew allocations, rigid gang-sizing rules, and fixed service fees (such as compulsory welfare and administrative surcharges) that do not reflect actual productivity or real work requirements42.  
* *Automation Friction:* As terminal operators install automated cargo handling equipment—including modern ship-to-shore cranes, twin-lift spreaders, and automated gate technologies—the need for manual labor on berths and yards has dropped significantly43. Yet terminal operators and stevedoring firms remain legally obligated to pay for full TKBM gang rosters, forcing them to carry redundant labor costs for work automated machinery performs faster and safer16.  
* *Resistance to Regulatory Reform:* Successive government attempts to revoke the 2011 decree and allow competing, corporate labor providers to operate have been met with strike threats and mass demonstrations outside the Port Authority offices, threatening to shut down port logistics32. This political deadlock leaves terminal operators unable to reform waterside handling workflows, perpetuating higher stevedoring costs and slower vessel work rates compared to regional benchmark ports44.

## **Strategic Governance and Operational Pathways**

Resolving the structural bottlenecks across the Tanjung Priok port ecosystem requires shifting from fragmented, bilateral fixes to an integrated systems-governance approach. While single-window digital platforms like the National Logistics Ecosystem (NLE) and automated terminal systems (TOS Nusantara) have improved data exchange, physical and institutional workflows remain constrained by legacy agreements and misaligned economic incentives11.  
Addressing these challenges calls for reforms centered on three main operational priorities:

### **1\. Regulatory Harmonization and Storage Tariff Reform**

Line ministries must align trade restrictions and import licensing requirements with actual border processing capacity. Implementing mandatory regulatory impact assessments before revising import restrictions (Lartas) will help prevent the sudden backlogs seen during recent trade policy shifts21.  
In parallel, terminal storage rate structures must be overhauled to remove the pricing loophole that makes customs-bonded yards (Lini 1\) cheaper than private inland warehouses, ensuring container yards are used for transit rather than long-term inventory storage9.

### **2\. Synchronized Landside and Rail Intermodal Infrastructure**

The Terminal Booking System (TBS) should be supported by dedicated off-dock truck staging areas (buffer parks) located along major port highway approaches, giving delayed trucks a place to wait without blocking city traffic3.  
To make intermodal freight viable, the government should eliminate double-handling fee structures and establish on-dock rail infrastructure directly connecting marine terminals to Cikarang Dry Port and broader West Java industrial parks, shifting freight off congested roads54.

### **3\. Institutional Labor Modernization**

The legacy labor model under the 2011 Tripartite Decree (SKB 2 Dirjen 1 Deputi) requires modern institutional reform39.  
Shifting from an exclusive cooperative labor monopoly to an open, regulated stevedoring framework will allow terminal operators to build professional, skilled workforces tailored to modern automated equipment, while ensuring fair wages, job protections, and safety standards for waterfront workers39.  
Through aligned regulatory policies, coordinated physical and digital systems, and modernized waterfront labor institutions, Tanjung Priok can resolve its operational bottlenecks, maintain stable sub-2.5-day dwelling times, and reinforce its position as an efficient, competitive maritime hub for global trade34.
