# **Operational Performance Analysis and Efficiency Benchmarking of Tanjung Priok Port as of September 30, 2026**

## **Baseline Operational Performance and System Architecture**

Tanjung Priok functions as the primary maritime logistics gateway of the Republic of Indonesia, processing more than 50% of the nation’s containerized foreign trade and serving as the primary consolidator for inter-island domestic freight1. Following the post-merger integration of PT Pelabuhan Indonesia (Pelindo) in late 2021, the port complex has undergone an operational transformation driven by terminal operating system standardization, physical infrastructure additions, and digital integration under the National Logistics Ecosystem (NLE)3. As of the close of the third quarter of 2026, the port complex handles an annualized throughput trajectory expanding beyond historical peaks, supported by the phased operationalization of the New Priok development and continuous productivity improvements across legacy terminals3.  
Annual container traffic through Tanjung Priok expanded from 7.60 million twenty-foot equivalent units (TEUs) in 2024—a 5.7% year-on-year increase—to 8.30 million TEUs in 20251. Alongside this container throughput, the complex processed 21.51 million tonnes of non-containerized cargo and approximately 860,000 completely built-up vehicle units through the Indonesia Kendaraan Terminal (IPCC)6. By September 30, 2026, volume indicators maintained this upward momentum.  
Operating subsidiaries such as IPC Terminal Petikemas (IPC TPK) reported an 8.0% year-on-year expansion through July 2026, handling 2.17 million TEUs, and surpassed 2.50 million TEUs across its operational areas through August 20267. This growth was anchored by a 15.2% volume increase within the core Tanjung Priok working areas9. Cumulative throughput at New Priok Container Terminal 1 (NPCT1) surpassed the 10-million-TEU threshold since its commissioning, demonstrating steady capacity absorption of the Kalibaru development3.  
Berth productivity across the harbor exhibits structural divergence between modernized deep-water facilities and older inner-harbor docks3. At NPCT1, equipped with super-post-Panamax rail-mounted quay cranes (RMQCs) featuring twin-lift and dual-hoist capabilities, gross crane productivity consistently registers between 30 and 32 moves per hour (box crane hour, BCH)3. This high crane intensity enables a 10,000-TEU container vessel to execute its cargo exchange in under 24 hours3.  
In contrast, legacy wharves within Jakarta International Container Terminal (JICT), TPK Koja, and Pelindo Regional 2 common-user berths operate at gross productivity rates between 22 and 26 BCH3. The operational cadence in these older sectors is constrained by equipment age, narrower aprons, and slower yard transfer cycles, where rubber-tyred gantry (RTG) cranes register handling cycles averaging 106 to 114 seconds per box move during vessel discharge sequences13.  
Vessel turnaround time (TRT) has stabilized near an average of 24.0 to 28.5 hours for scheduled international container liners, down from historic benchmarks of 36 to 48 hours3. This enables multi-carrier alliances to maintain scheduled weekly rotations across Southeast and East Asian loops3. However, an operational divide persists between international trade and domestic inter-island shipping16.  
Official port monitoring via the Inaportnet digital portal reveals that while foreign-flagged, cellular container vessels maintain average port stays well below 3 days, domestic cargo and feeder ships frequently experience port turnarounds exceeding 3 to 4 days16. Domestic feeder operations are hindered by non-cellular hull designs, loose breakbulk stowage, unstandardized booking schedules, and lower priority in waterside resource allocations16.  
Container dwelling time within the bonded terminal boundaries has decreased from historical levels of 5.5 to 7.0 days down to a stabilized average of 2.54 to 2.67 days3. This metric is split across three operational phases: pre-customs clearance, customs clearance, and post-clearance cargo uplift20. Pre-clearance accounts for approximately 0.85 to 0.95 days, facilitated by mandatory 24-hour advance manifest submissions via the Indonesia National Single Window (INSW)20.  
Customs clearance across the dominant Green Channel (Jalur Hijau), representing roughly 80% to 85% of total import consignments, averages 0.35 to 0.45 days20. Post-clearance gate collection averages 1.15 to 1.35 days, governed by commercial cargo-uplift dynamics and consignee delivery preferences20.  
Yard utilization, monitored via the Yard Occupancy Rate (YOR), typically operates within a manageable band of 68% to 76% across the major container terminals3. However, during seasonal volume peaks or prolonged holidays, import yard blocks can exceed 80% YOR3. When occupancy passes this threshold, stacking density increases non-productive re-handling maneuvers by RTGs, lengthening retrieval cycles13. To alleviate yard congestion during import surges, the Port Authority (Kantor Kesyahbandaran dan Otoritas Pelabuhan / KSOP Utama Tanjung Priok) enforces overflow policies that route empty containers and long-stay boxes to satellite yards and alternative terminals, including Patimban23.  
Landside drayage performance is shaped by gate automation and terminal queuing dynamics24. Under the Single Truck Identification Data (STID) system, more than 36,000 heavy container prime movers are registered to operate across Tanjung Priok25. When trucks arrive within scheduled appointment windows, Truck Turnaround Time (TTT)—measured from terminal gate entry, across RTG yard bay spotting, to automated gate checkout—averages 48.7 to 52.4 minutes24.  
However, when cargo cut-off deadlines coincide with uncontrolled drayage surges, unscheduled arrivals overwhelm access plazas, extending turnaround times beyond 90 minutes24. These landside delays disrupt equipment dispatch and cause truck queues to back up onto municipal access arteries24.

## **Comparative Performance Benchmarking: Domestic and Regional Transshipment Hubs**

Evaluating Tanjung Priok against domestic gateways and regional transshipment hubs highlights its role as an origin-and-destination (O/D) trade portal rather than a pure maritime transshipment hub3. Domestically, Tanjung Priok serves the consumer population of Greater Jakarta and the manufacturing export zones of West Java, handling over half the country's international container traffic2.  
Tanjung Perak in Surabaya serves as Indonesia’s secondary maritime hub, processing 4.07 to 4.25 million TEUs annually31. It handles import-export trade for East Java while serving as the primary transshipment stepping-stone for eastern domestic routes14.  
Makassar New Port operates as the primary eastern regional hub, processing approximately 0.85 to 1.10 million TEUs with shorter vessel port stays due to smaller call sizes7. Patimban Port in Subang serves as a relief valve for the eastern industrial corridor, handling roughly 0.15 to 0.35 million TEUs alongside automotive Ro-Ro traffic30.  
Internationally, the Malacca Strait corridor is anchored by PSA Singapore, Port of Tanjung Pelepas (PTP), and Port Klang33. Singapore processes between 39.0 and 43.9 million TEUs annually, while Port Klang handles 13.5 to 14.5 million TEUs and PTP manages 10.5 to 13.0 million TEUs31. Transshipment containers account for 60% to 90% of total volume at these facilities29.  
In the World Bank and S\&P Global Market Intelligence *Container Port Performance Index* (CPPI 2023, published in 2024), Tanjung Priok ranked 23rd globally out of 405 assessed ports (and 20th under statistical matrix scoring), placing 13th among East and Southeast Asian ports15. This represents a significant improvement from its 2022 ranking of 281st out of 348 ports, when post-pandemic cargo surges caused extensive waterside delays39.  
By comparison, Tanjung Perak ranked 101st to 105th globally, while regional transshipment centers maintained leading positions: Tanjung Pelepas ranked 4th to 6th, Singapore ranked 19th, and Port Klang placed 27th to 28th15.

The performance variance between Tanjung Priok and hubs like Singapore or Tanjung Pelepas reflects fundamental differences in operational profiles29. Transshipment centers move containers primarily from ship to ship within bonded terminal areas, bypassing import customs inspections, regulatory quarantine checks, and off-dock road delivery29. This pure transshipment model enables crane productivities exceeding 34 moves per hour and short dwell times of 1.0 to 1.5 days41.  
Tanjung Priok, in contrast, operates primarily as an import-export gateway where over 80% of containers must clear national customs and be hauled over urban road networks3. Consequently, landside gate capacity, road traffic patterns, and customs examination systems heavily influence Tanjung Priok’s waterside performance and container turnaround times21.

## **Structural and Operational Drivers of Performance Divergence**

### **Waterside Infrastructure and Equipment Choreography**

Performance across Tanjung Priok varies due to differences in physical infrastructure and cargo-handling superstructures2. At NPCT1, the 300-meter-wide outer channel and \-16.8-meter berth depth allow two-way navigation and can accommodate ultra-large container vessels of up to 18,000 TEUs3. The terminal's electric rail-mounted quay cranes feature twin-lift spreaders and advanced anti-sway controls that maintain productivities above 30 BCH3.  
In contrast, the inner basins serving JICT, TPK Koja, and Pelindo Regional 2 terminals have water depths ranging from \-10.0 to \-14.0 meters, limiting access for deep-draft mainliners during lower tidal windows2.  
Equipment capabilities also diverge in yard operations. In older container yards, diesel and electric RTGs average cycle times of 106 to 114 seconds per box during vessel unloading13. When vessel discharge rates peak, these handling cycles can lead to internal truck queuing along terminal transfer lanes if head-truck dispatches are not balanced with yard crane positioning13.

### **Digital Integration and Operating System Standardization**

Following Pelindo’s corporate consolidation, terminal operations transitioned from disparate legacy systems to a standardized software architecture4. Container terminals run on the Pelindo Terminal Operating System Container (PTOS-C), while multi-purpose and breakbulk docks use the Pelindo Terminal Operating System Multipurpose (PTOS-M)5.  
These platforms integrate directly with the Ministry of Transportation’s Inaportnet for vessel clearance and with the Phinnisi marine platform for pilotage and tug scheduling managed by PT Jasa Armada Indonesia (IPCM)5. This integrated architecture has reduced vessel idle times at anchorage by improving coordination between marine pilotage and berth readiness3.  
Data sharing also connects terminal systems to the National Logistics Ecosystem (NLE), enabling automatic generation of gate-in permits upon digital customs release (Surat Persetujuan Pengeluaran Barang / SPPB)5.  
However, integration friction remains between terminal operating systems and third-party logistics software. Delays in transmitting electronic delivery orders (e-DO) between shipping agents and freight forwarders can stall automated gate clearances, causing import containers to linger in terminal stacks despite receiving customs approval17.

### **Regulatory Architecture, Customs Inspections, and Dwelling Metrics**

Dwelling time at Tanjung Priok is determined by administrative procedures spanning pre-clearance, customs inspection, and post-clearance cargo release20. While pre-clearance averages roughly 0.9 days and automated Green Channel processing clears imports in under half a day, significant administrative bottlenecks persist in the physical inspection Red Channel (Jalur Merah)20.  
Containers flagged for physical examination and quarantine checks—typically 10% to 15% of inbound imports—must be transferred from main yard blocks to dedicated inspection facilities, such as Tempat Penimbunan Fisik Terpadu (TPFT) Graha Segara or CDC Banda21. This transfer requires secondary terminal handling, administrative gate checks, unstuffing, physical verification, and re-stacking, extending dwell times for audited containers to 5 to 8 days21.  
These delays are compounded by commercial storage arbitrage21. Importers and consignees frequently find it more economical to absorb port storage fees during the early demurrage tier than to pay local warehousing, transportation, and double-handling charges at off-dock facilities21.  
This dynamic leads cargo owners to use terminal yards as temporary holding areas, driving up yard occupancy rates and forcing terminal operators to conduct non-productive container shuffles during retrieval cycles13.

### **Hinterland Connectivity and Intermodal Flow**

Landside hinterland access remains the primary bottleneck for Tanjung Priok's logistics performance27. Over-the-road trucking handles roughly 95% of total container transport to and from the port22. Although the 34-kilometer Cibitung-Cilincing Toll Road (JTCC) connects the port directly to the eastern industrial corridor of Cikarang, Karawang, and Cibitung, it remains underutilized47.  
The JTCC accommodates approximately 8,700 vehicles per day—well below its design capacity—primarily due to higher toll rates and an absence of tariff integration with the connecting Jakarta Outer Ring Road (JORR) and Jakarta-Cikampek highway networks27.  
As a result, many trucking operators divert containers onto non-toll municipal roads, including Jalan Raya Cakung-Cilincing (Jalan Cacing), Jalan Jampea, and the Marunda corridor27. Heavy container trucks mix with local urban traffic along these surface routes, slowing average truck speeds to 5–10 km/h during peak morning and evening hours27.  
Meanwhile, rail freight connectivity via Cikarang Dry Port (CDP) provides scheduled services carrying up to 30 railcars (60 TEUs) per train into the port rail terminal22. However, intermodal rail accounts for less than 5% of total container movements22. Modal share is constrained by intermediate shunting and drayage transfers between the rail terminal and ship berths, double-handling fees, grade-crossing delays, and cost competition from road transport22.

## **Primary Operational Bottlenecks**

### **Landside Chokepoint Dynamics: The Arterial-Tollway Disconnect**

The most consequential operational bottleneck at Tanjung Priok is the mismatch between arterial road congestion and tollway utilization27. With more than 36,000 container trucks operating in the port's transport network, vehicle arrivals concentrate heavily between 10:00–14:00 and 18:00–22:00, overwhelming access roads around Pos 8, Pos 9, and the Cakung-Cilincing corridor25.  
Because toll tariffs between the JTCC and connecting ring roads are not integrated, trucking companies often run empty returns and unladen chassis along local municipal roads27. This creates chronic traffic congestion in northern Jakarta, causes export boxes to miss designated vessel loading windows, and reduces average fleet efficiency from a target of 2.5 revenue trips per truck per day to fewer than 1.2 trips24.

### **The Red Channel and Pre-/Post-Clearance Storage Arbitrage**

Despite reductions in average dwell times to roughly 2.6 days, physical customs examinations (Jalur Merah) continue to strain yard operations18. Shifting red-channel containers from primary stacks to off-dock inspection areas (TPFT) requires secondary handling by terminal trucks, inspection gate verifications, unstuffing, and re-stacking43.  
This process extends dwell times for inspected cargo by 3 to 5 days and diverts terminal equipment from standard vessel discharge and loading operations13.  
At the same time, commercial consignees frequently use terminal yards for short-term storage when off-dock warehousing costs exceed early-tier terminal demurrage fees21. This practice ties up ground slots in primary import blocks, forcing RTG operators into non-productive container shuffles during retrievals and extending truck service times across terminal bays13.

### **Operational Disconnect Between Modern Gateway Basins and Domestic Multi-Purpose Berths**

Tanjung Priok exhibits an operational divide between its modern international container terminals and legacy domestic handling wharves3. Terminals like NPCT1 and JICT utilize automated gate systems, optical character recognition (OCR), RTG position detection, and integrated computerized TOS controls2. In contrast, legacy domestic berths operated by Pelindo Regional 2 and common-user facilities handle mixed feeder vessels using low-capacity rail cranes or shipboard lifting gear11.  
Domestic operations frequently experience delayed pilot boarding, slow line handling, manual stowage planning, and inconsistent container yard tracking16. This causes vessel turnarounds for domestic traffic to exceed 72 hours, occupying inner-harbor berths and creating ripple-effect delays across navigation channels14.

### **Terminal Gate Wave Arrivals and the Truck Appointment Void**

Although the STID system provides visibility into the port's commercial trucking fleet, truck arrivals at terminal gates remain uncoordinated24. Processing a single vehicle at an automated RFID gate takes roughly 2.0 to 3.5 minutes24. However, uncontrolled truck arrivals during export cut-off windows create long vehicle queues that spill backward through terminal gates and onto main access arteries24.  
Without a dynamic, port-wide Truck Appointment System (TAS) linked to yard crane availability and equipment spotting schedules, yard cranes oscillate between periods of low activity and intense congestion24. During peak congestion windows, truck waiting times within the yard can rise from baseline levels of under 48 minutes to over 90 minutes24.

## **Measurable Improvement Hypotheses for Business Immersion Validation**

To support the K36 Business Immersion study, four testable hypotheses have been developed. Each targets an operational bottleneck using specific mechanisms, clear baseline metrics, defined targets, and established validation protocols.

### **Hypothesis 1: Dynamic Gate Appointment Integration via STID-Linked Terminal Slot-Booking**

Unscheduled truck arrivals create severe congestion at Pos 8/9 entry gates and inside terminal yard blocks during export cut-off windows, pushing unmanaged truck turnaround times beyond 90 minutes24. Integrating the Single Truck Identification Data (STID) platform with a mandatory, hourly Truck Appointment System (TAS) across JICT, TPK Koja, and NPCT1 can smooth vehicle arrival flows24. This enables TOS dispatch algorithms to pre-stage outbound containers and allocate yard RTG resources to match incoming truck volumes24.  
It is hypothesized that implementing a dynamic, mandatory STID-linked Truck Appointment System (TAS) with hourly slot windows will compress average in-terminal Truck Turnaround Time (TTT) from 48.72 minutes down to 35.00 minutes or less, while reducing peak gate queue lengths by 45%.  
Baseline parameters register a mean TTT of 48.72 to 52.40 minutes, gate-in processing times of 120 to 180 seconds, and an arrival coefficient of variation of 0.6524. Target performance parameters require a mean TTT of 35.00 minutes or less, gate processing under 90 seconds, and an arrival coefficient of variation below 0.30.  
The immersion validation protocol requires:

> 1. Extracting automated gate time-stamps (gate-in to gate-out) from PTOS-C logs across participating terminals over a 30-day trial period.  
> 2. Segmenting turnaround performance into appointment-compliant versus ad-hoc arrivals to isolate waiting-time differentials.  
> 3. Conducting video-analytics assessments at gate complexes during peak morning and evening windows (10:00–14:00 and 18:00–22:00) to measure queue dissipation rates.  
> 4. Evaluating statistical correlations between hourly gate throughput, yard RTG cycle times, and truck wait durations within terminal bays13.

### **Hypothesis 2: Customs Pre-Arrival Clearance and Off-Dock Behandle Shifting via NLE**

Physical inspection containers (Jalur Merah) and related quarantine holds remain inside terminal stacks for an average of 5 to 7 days21. These containers require secondary moves to on-dock inspection areas (TPFT Graha Segara), creating yard congestion and increasing overall port dwell times21. Using the National Logistics Ecosystem (NLE) to automate pre-arrival documentation and enable bonded transfers of Red Channel cargo directly to bonded off-dock inspection depots (such as Cikarang Dry Port or dedicated Marunda buffer facilities) bypasses congested terminal stacks5. This relieves pressure on main terminal yards and allows dedicated inspection handling21.  
It is hypothesized that executing pre-arrival customs clearances through the NLE and transferring Red Channel import containers directly to bonded off-dock inspection depots under electronic seals (e-seals) within 12 hours of vessel discharge will reduce average Red Channel dwell times from 6.20 days to 3.50 days or less, thereby lowering terminal Yard Occupancy Rates (YOR) by 6.0 percentage points.  
Baseline values show an average Red Channel dwell time of 6.20 days, port-wide aggregate dwell time of 2.54 to 2.67 days, and terminal YOR running between 72% and 76%3. Target metrics specify Red Channel dwell times of 3.50 days or less, aggregate port dwell times below 2.10 days, and terminal YOR settling between 66% and 70%.  
The immersion validation protocol requires:

> 1. Extracting import clearance data from CEISA 4.0, INSW, and PTOS-C to measure elapsed times across pre-clearance, physical inspection, and gate exit.  
> 2. Comparing dwell times and transfer costs for cargo inspected at on-dock facilities (TPFT Graha Segara) versus shipments routed to bonded off-dock depots22.  
> 3. Calculating the net reduction in non-productive RTG container shuffles achieved by moving inspection boxes out of primary yard stacks13.  
> 4. Modeling terminal yard capacity gains and spatial utilization profiles across varying import volume scenarios.

### **Hypothesis 3: Automated Dual-Cycle Crane Sequencing and Dynamic Internal Drayage Pooling**

Legacy berths at JICT, TPK Koja, and Regional 2 terminals frequently run separate discharge and load cycles12. This practice increases empty internal truck moves, limits gross crane productivity to 22–26 moves per hour, and extends vessel port stays3. Upgrading the PTOS-C platform to support real-time dual-cycling enables quay cranes to discharge an import container and load an export box in a single hoist trolley cycle9. Connecting this process to dynamic yard crane staging reduces empty truck travel and improves berth productivity13.  
It is hypothesized that deploying an automated dual-cycling sequencing module within PTOS-C paired with dynamic internal head-truck pooling will raise gross quay crane productivity across legacy international berths from 24.50 BCH to 32.00 BCH or higher, reducing average international vessel port stays by 18%.  
Baseline parameters show gross crane productivities averaging 24.50 BCH, mean international vessel turnarounds of 26.50 hours, and internal truck empty travel rates (deadheading) of 42%3. Target metrics require gross crane productivity of 32.00 BCH or higher, vessel turnaround times of 21.50 hours or less, and internal truck deadheading reduced to 22% or below.  
The immersion validation protocol requires:

> 1. Conducting operational trials across paired container berths handling vessels in the 4,000 to 8,000 TEU range.  
> 2. Comparing operations using standard single-cycle sequencing against those running automated PTOS-C dual-cycle sequencing.  
> 3. Measuring gross moves per crane hour, crane idle time waiting for yard trucks, and internal truck cycle durations using equipment telemetry12.  
> 4. Logging total vessel service time from first line secure to all lines cast off to assess impact on vessel turnaround times15.

### **Hypothesis 4: Multimodal Shift via Toll Tariff Harmonization and Intermodal Rail Subsidies**

The Cibitung-Cilincing Toll Road (JTCC) remains underutilized at only \~8,700 trucks per day, while the Cikarang Dry Port rail corridor handles less than 5% of container traffic22. This underutilization directs heavy truck traffic onto local roads, causing severe congestion in the Cakung-Cilincing corridor and generating delays for port deliveries27. Implementing integrated toll discounts across the JTCC, JORR, and port access highways—paired with reduced shunting fees at terminal rail sidings—can balance freight economics22. Lowering these transfer costs incentivizes operators to shift traffic away from congested urban roads and onto highway and rail alternatives22.  
It is hypothesized that introducing an integrated toll tariff on the JTCC alongside a 25% reduction in terminal rail transfer fees will increase daily heavy truck volume on the JTCC to 16,000 vehicles or more, double rail throughput via Cikarang Dry Port, and raise average truck speeds along the Cakung-Cilincing corridor by 35%.  
Baseline values show JTCC daily heavy truck traffic of approximately 8,700 vehicles, monthly rail throughput via CDP of roughly 4,500 TEUs, and average peak truck speeds along Jalan Cacing of 11.4 km/h22. Target metrics require JTCC daily volume of 16,000 heavy vehicles or more, monthly rail volume reaching 9,500 TEUs or higher, and arterial corridor travel speeds improving to 18.0 km/h or faster during peak windows.  
The immersion validation protocol requires:

> 1. Collecting vehicle classification and toll transaction data from PT CTP Tollways and BPJT to measure diversion rates47.  
> 2. Tracking monthly rail cargo manifests and container transfers between Cikarang Dry Port and Tanjung Priok railhead sidings22.  
> 3. Measuring truck transit speeds and queue lengths along the Cakung-Cilincing, Pos 8, and Pos 9 corridors using GPS telemetry and traffic sensors27.  
> 4. Surveying commercial trucking fleets and logistics providers (Aptrindo/ALFI) to evaluate route choice sensitivities regarding toll pricing, fuel consumption, and vehicle turnaround times8.

## **Strategic Synthesis and Implementation Roadmap**

Tanjung Priok’s operational performance through the third quarter of 2026 demonstrates significant modernization across its waterside operations, reflected in container throughput reaching 8.30 million TEUs in 2025 and sustained growth into 20266. Digital platform standardization through PTOS-C, PTOS-M, Phinnisi, Inaportnet, and the National Logistics Ecosystem has improved operational visibility, reduced berth waiting times, and shortened average container dwell times to 2.54–2.67 days3. These performance gains contributed to the port securing the 23rd position globally in the World Bank’s Container Port Performance Index15.  
However, maintaining this operational level requires addressing bottlenecks along the port's landside interface27. While waterside handling at deep-water berths like NPCT1 operates at standards comparable to regional transshipment centers, landside operations face ongoing challenges from urban road congestion, an underutilized hinterland toll network, and delays in physical customs inspections3.  
Addressing these challenges requires better alignment between waterside operations and landside transport networks27. Priority initiatives include integrating truck appointment systems with STID controls, routing Red Channel customs inspections to bonded off-dock facilities, adopting dual-cycle crane sequencing, and harmonizing intermodal toll and rail tariffs9. By resolving these landside friction points, Tanjung Priok can protect its berth productivity gains, lower supply chain costs for Indonesian manufacturers, and support national economic integration as regional port volumes continue to expand3.
