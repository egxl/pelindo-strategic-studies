# **Strategic Assessment of PT Pelabuhan Indonesia (Persero): Post-Merger Consolidation, Infrastructure Modernization, and Network Dynamics as of September 30, 2026**

## **Post-Merger Corporate Evolution and Governance Trajectory**

The consolidation of Indonesia’s state-owned port sector on October 1, 2021, executed under Government Regulation (PP) No. 101/2021, dissolved the legacy regional monopolies—PT Pelabuhan Indonesia I, II, III, and IV (Persero)—into a single unified operating enterprise: PT Pelabuhan Indonesia (Persero), commercially designated as Pelindo1. Before this integration, the four entities operated as independent, regionally bounded authorities with fragmented capital programs, incompatible software stacks, and non-standardized marine tariffs1. This historic fragmentation created pronounced operational friction across the national supply chain, contributing to elevated domestic logistics costs and inefficient hub-and-spoke cargo consolidation2. The merger sought to establish an integrated commercial operator capable of centralizing capital allocation, standardizing quayside and marine services, lowering container dwelling times, and driving national multimodal connectivity1.  
The corporation's strategic development across the 2021–2026 horizon has evolved across three structured phases:  
The initial transition, spanning 2021 through 2022, focused on legal alignment, commercial stabilization, and structural unbundling1. During this stage, Pelindo consolidated overlapping port infrastructure and reorganized operations from geographical groupings into specialized functional sub-holdings1. Management established common operational accounting, harmonized personnel classifications, and restructured legacy syndicated debt facilities into a centralized corporate balance sheet1.  
Between 2023 and 2024, the enterprise executed systematic operational standardization and digital harmonization1. Regional operating differences were phased out in favor of unified terminal operating applications, centralized vessel traffic protocols, and streamlined maritime service tariffs6. Pelindo also rationalized its non-core and sub-tier operating units, merging internal service providers to eliminate duplicated corporate overhead and intra-group procurement redundancies1.  
Beginning in 2025 and accelerating through 2026 under the *Rencana Jangka Panjang Perusahaan* (RJPP) 2025–2029, Pelindo expanded its operating focus beyond the marine terminal gate to emphasize hinterland infrastructure, intermodal transport, and industrial zone integration8. The enterprise actively restructured its logistics assets to establish dedicated freight links between deep-water berths and inland manufacturing centers, while implementing standardized sustainability programs across its primary commercial terminals10.  
Corporate governance underwent a significant structural realignment in early 202613. Following the multi-year tenure of President Director Arif Suhartono and Operations Director Putut Sri Muljanto—who guided the enterprise through its legal integration and initial operational stabilization—the government, via the newly empowered sovereign wealth institution Badan Pengelola Investasi Daya Anagata Nusantara (BPI Danantara), overhauled the executive suite13. Achmad Muchtasyar was appointed President Director (*Direktur Utama*), supported by Prasetyadi as Operations Director (*Direktur Operasi*), while the formal position of Vice President Director was eliminated13.  
This restructuring aligned with Danantara's mandate to centralize state-owned enterprise governance, enforce strict capital allocation, accelerate the divestment of non-core balance sheet assets, and increase annual dividend remittances to the state15.

## **Organizational Structure, Operating Model, and Subsidiary Portfolio**

Pelindo operates under a matrix governance framework that separates territorial asset stewardship from specialized operational execution1. Corporate headquarters functions as a strategic holding company responsible for capital expenditure financing, broad regulatory compliance, investor relations, and macro-planning1. Operating responsibilities below the holding company are structured into four Regional Divisions and four Functional Sub-Holdings1.  
The four Regional Divisions—Regional 1 (overseeing the Malacca Strait, North Sumatra, and Riau corridors), Regional 2 (managing Western Java, South Sumatra, and West Kalimantan), Regional 3 (directing Central and East Java, Bali, Nusa Tenggara, and Southern Kalimantan), and Regional 4 (administering Sulawesi, Maluku, Papua, and East Kalimantan)—serve primarily as landlord asset stewards1. They manage long-term state concession contracts with the Ministry of Transportation's Port Authorities (*Kantor Otoritas Pelabuhan*), maintain municipal relations, ensure basic physical security, and oversee port zone land banks across more than 71 commercial ports spanning 32 provinces1.  
Commercial terminal operations, marine piloting, equipment engineering, and value-added logistics services are managed by four functional sub-holdings:

### **PT Pelindo Terminal Petikemas (SPTP)**

Headquartered in Surabaya, SPTP oversees the group's container terminal operations2. SPTP exercises operating and equity control over dedicated container entities, including PT IPC Terminal Petikemas (IPC TPK), PT Terminal Petikemas Surabaya (TPS), PT Berlian Jasa Terminal Indonesia (BJTI), and flagship joint ventures such as PT Jakarta International Container Terminal (JICT) and PT New Priok Container Terminal One (NPCT1)24. SPTP focuses on standardizing quayside container handling productivity, lifting hourly box crane rates, and deploying centralized operating systems across primary and secondary container yards2.

### **PT Pelindo Multi Terminal (SPMT)**

Headquartered in Medan, SPMT manages non-containerized cargo assets across the archipelago2. Its commercial scope includes dry bulk (coal, iron ore, clinker, grains), liquid bulk (crude palm oil, refined edible oils, chemical commodities), and breakbulk project cargo30. SPMT controls PT Pelabuhan Tanjung Priok (PTP Nonpetikemas) and holds a 71.3% majority stake in the publicly listed automotive terminal operator PT Indonesia Kendaraan Terminal Tbk (IPCC)30. SPMT is tasked with eliminating manual cargo handling processes, replacing fragmented local contracts with long-term industrial master service agreements, and digitizing non-container quayside operations6.

### **PT Pelindo Jasa Maritim (SPJM)**

Headquartered in Makassar, SPJM manages integrated marine support and technical services across all commercial waterways4. The sub-holding directs harbor tug towage, marine pilotage dispatch, offshore ship support, dredging operations, utilities provision, and port equipment maintenance4. Its primary operating companies include PT Pelindo Marine Service and the publicly listed marine support firm PT Jasa Armada Indonesia Tbk (IPCM)33. SPJM's core operational priority is deploying unified vessel assistance scheduling systems and expanding certified shore-to-ship power distribution6.

### **PT Pelindo Sinergi Lokaseva (PSL)**

Headquartered in Jakarta, this company was originally formed at the merger as PT Pelindo Solusi Logistik (SPSL) and formally rebranded as PT Pelindo Sinergi Lokaseva in February 202610. The rebranding marked an operational shift away from commoditized freight forwarding toward port-centric industrial park infrastructure, dry port development, integrated inland container depots, and high-speed port hinterland access routes10. PSL oversees PT Multi Terminal Indonesia (MTI) and freight corridor assets including PT Akses Pelabuhan Indonesia (API) and PT Cibitung Tanjung Priok Port Tollways (PT CTP), the concession holder of the Cibitung-Cilincing Toll Road (JTCC)7.  
In mid-2026, under BPI Danantara's broader state-owned enterprise consolidation program, PSL served as the surviving operating platform to absorb seven previously fragmented state-owned logistics units into an integrated logistics network40.

## **National Port Network Architecture and Operational Performance**

Pelindo organizes its national network into a hub-and-spoke operational structure designed to concentrate high-density container and bulk cargo flows at primary deep-water hubs while maintaining scheduled feeder connections across peripheral islands1.  
The Western Maritime Gateway is anchored by Belawan and the deep-water Kuala Tanjung port complex in North Sumatra1. Situated along the Malacca Strait, this corridor captures regional traffic while channeling liquid bulk and agricultural exports directly to international markets1.  
The Central Industrial Maritime Corridor is centered around Tanjung Priok in Jakarta and Tanjung Perak in Surabaya, which handle the majority of national consumer imports, industrial machinery, and processed manufacturing exports2. This corridor is supported by key secondary hubs: Tanjung Emas in Semarang handles growing export volumes from Central Java's expanding manufacturing base, while the deep-water Kijing Terminal in Mempawah, West Kalimantan, operates as a specialized export terminal for bauxite, refined alumina, and palm oil products31.  
The Eastern Maritime Gateway is anchored by Makassar New Port (MNP) in South Sulawesi, which functions as the principal transshipment and consolidation hub for Eastern Indonesia1. Feeder vessels from secondary ports in Maluku, Papua, East Nusa Tenggara, and North Sulawesi (Bitung) transfer cargo at MNP onto larger domestic trunk line vessels headed to Java, or onto regional international services, lowering overall sailing legs across the archipelago2.  
Consolidated throughput has expanded steadily since the merger46. Pelindo manages approximately 95% of Indonesia's commercial container traffic, making it one of the ten largest port operators globally by volume2.  
Group-wide container throughput grew from 17.3 million TEUs in 2022 to 18.8 million TEUs in 2024, representing an average annual growth rate of roughly 7%43. Operating volume trends across 2025 and 2026 indicate sustained annual growth of 4% to 5%, with full-year 2026 volume on track to reach 20.2 to 20.5 million TEUs47. SPTP processed 12.48 million TEUs through its consolidated and affiliated terminals in 202449.  
Subsidiary units showed similar operational expansion: IPC TPK handled 2.51 million TEUs through August 2026—an 8.37% year-on-year increase compared to 2.31 million TEUs over the same period in 2025—driven by double-digit traffic growth at regional gateways such as Teluk Bayur (+14.6%), Tanjung Priok (+13.2%), and Panjang (+10.9%)27.  
Gross vessel traffic handled across Pelindo’s commercial wharves reached 1.402 billion Gross Tonnage (GT) in 2024, tracking above 1.45 billion GT through 2025 and 202647. Non-containerized dry and liquid bulk handling similarly expanded, supported by higher commodity flows through specialized facilities such as Kijing Terminal, which recorded 3.27 million tons of bulk throughput in the first half of 2026 alone31.  
Financially, Pelindo maintains an investment-grade balance sheet supported by steady cash generation from international container handling, where tariffs are denominated in or linked to the US dollar5. Fitch Ratings affirmed Pelindo’s Long-Term Foreign-Currency Issuer Default Rating at 'BBB' with a Stable Outlook in mid-2025, matching Indonesia's sovereign credit rating20.  
Fitch highlighted the company's manageable leverage profile, noting an adjusted net debt-to-EBITDA ratio of approximately 3.5x, which compares favorably to international port peers like DP World20. The company generates EBITDA margins of roughly 30% on operating revenues exceeding Rp 33 trillion, reporting net profits of Rp 4.12 trillion in 2023, Rp 4.25 trillion in 2024, and Rp 1.6 trillion in the first half of 202518.  
Total assets exceed Rp 118 trillion, against long-term liabilities of roughly Rp 53 to Rp 55 trillion5. While this capital structure supports corporate financial stability, maintaining this leverage profile requires careful capital discipline given the enterprise's heavy capital expenditure commitments and increasing annual dividend targets from BPI Danantara, which were set at Rp 1.07 trillion for fiscal year 2024 and targeted higher for 2025 and 202618.

## **Transformation Programs: Digital Integration and Decarbonization**

Pelindo's post-merger operational program focuses on two main operational initiatives: replacing fragmented legacy software with unified digital platforms, and systematically decarbonizing quayside and marine terminal operations1.  
Before the merger, the four regional port operators ran more than a dozen incompatible IT systems, leaving shipping lines without unified visibility over berth availability, container locations, and billing data1. Between 2022 and 2026, Pelindo addressed this by rolling out three standardized software platforms across its terminal network6:

* **TOS Nusantara:** A proprietary Terminal Operating System deployed across SPTP’s container terminals28. TOS Nusantara coordinates quayside container cranes, plans yard stacking using predictive algorithms, and automates gate check-ins56. By mid-2026, the system was operational across all primary container terminals, increasing crane moves per hour and reducing vessel berth times by 20% to 30%32.  
* **PTOS-M (Pelindo Terminal Operating System Multipurpose):** Launched on the merger's second anniversary, PTOS-M provides a unified operating system for SPMT’s non-container terminals6. The platform standardizes planning, job order tracking, and billing for dry bulk, liquid bulk, and breakbulk cargo, replacing manual paper records with real-time operational tracking6.  
* **Phinisi:** A centralized vessel operations platform that manages marine services from pilot boarding to mooring6. Integrated with the Ministry of Transportation's Inaportnet, Phinisi standardizes tug dispatching, pilot scheduling, and marine utility billing across SPJM facilities, reducing idle vessel waiting times in commercial harbor waters6.

On the landside, Pelindo deployed the Single Truck Identification Data (STID) system alongside the Terminal Booking System (TBS) across major urban ports, including Tanjung Priok and Tanjung Perak37. STID assigns unique digital profiles to verified trucks, while TBS regulates gate arrival schedules to reduce peak-hour congestion on access roads37.  
Decarbonization efforts are aligned with Indonesia's national Net Zero Emission (NZE) 2060 target and the Ministry of Transportation's Green and Smart Port framework, which aims to certify 149 ports nationwide, including 112 Pelindo facilities12. Key decarbonization programs include:

* **Handling Equipment Electrification:** Replacing diesel Rubber-Tyred Gantry (RTG) cranes with electric RTGs (e-RTGs), deploying automated electric yard tractors at flagship facilities, and installing rooftop solar photovoltaic (PLTS) systems on port warehouses to power cold-storage yards41.  
* **Shore-to-Ship Power (Onshore Power Supply / OPS):** In partnership with state power utility PT PLN (Persero), Pelindo has installed over 27 MVA of shore connection capacity across primary ports in Java, Sumatra, Kalimantan, and Bali-Nusa Tenggara34. This infrastructure allows berthed vessels to shut down auxiliary diesel generators and run on grid electricity, significantly cutting particulate matter, sulfur dioxide, and carbon emissions in port areas33.  
* **Sustainable Port Development:** Terminal Teluk Lamong (TTL) at Tanjung Perak serves as the group's operational benchmark for sustainable port design12. TTL features fully electrified automated stacking cranes, shore power infrastructure, and low-emission internal terminal tractors, serving as an operating model for the phased modernization of older terminal basins across the country12.

## **Macroeconomic Role in Indonesia’s Logistics Ecosystem**

Indonesia’s logistics costs historically stood between 23% and 24% of Gross Domestic Product (GDP), presenting a long-standing drag on industrial competitiveness compared to regional peers like Vietnam (16% to 18%), Thailand (13% to 14%), and Malaysia (11% to 12%)3.  
Cross-ministerial studies conducted by Bappenas established an updated national logistics cost baseline of 14.29% of GDP, setting a strategic target to reduce this figure below 10% by 20453. Pelindo serves as a primary state instrument for achieving this reduction1.  
Pelindo influences national logistics efficiency across four main areas:  
First, the enterprise has reduced maritime transit times and port stays by standardizing cargo-handling processes6. Consolidating marine pilotage dispatch, berth scheduling, and quayside crane operations under single sub-holdings reduced domestic port turnaround times from four to seven days down to 2.5 to 3.0 days at primary hub ports63. Faster vessel turnarounds lower unit shipping costs for domestic shipping lines, directly reducing inter-island freight rates1.  
Second, Pelindo provides essential terminal handling and yard storage infrastructure for the national Maritime Highway (*Tol Laut*) program61. Managing facilities across 39 scheduled public-service and commercial shipping loops, Pelindo applies preferential tariffs and guarantees priority berthing for state-subsidized routes serving remote areas in Eastern Indonesia, helping narrow price disparities for basic consumer goods and construction materials between Java and outer islands61.  
Third, Pelindo enforces cabotage regulations under Law No. 17/2008 by providing the quayside and terminal infrastructure required by domestic shipping lines to aggregate inter-island cargo2. Dedicated domestic container berths at Tanjung Priok, Tanjung Perak, and Makassar New Port enable local carriers to feed regional cargo into national trunk lines, consolidating export volumes onto deep-sea direct vessels and reducing dependence on foreign transshipment hubs like Singapore and Port Klang2.  
Fourth, through its rebranded logistics arm Pelindo Sinergi Lokaseva, the enterprise is expanding beyond traditional quayside operations into direct hinterland connectivity10. By investing in freight tollways, dry ports, and inland container depots, Pelindo connects deep-water quaysides directly to inland manufacturing clusters, addressing landside transit delays that historically accounted for a significant share of total supply chain costs3.

## **Core Strategic Challenges Facing Pelindo**

Despite measurable post-merger efficiency gains, Pelindo faces three major structural challenges that affect its capital allocation, network planning, and long-term operating margins.

### **Challenge 1: Hinterland Freight Inefficiencies and Overreliance on Highway Drayage**

More than 85% of import and export freight moving through Indonesia’s major commercial ports relies entirely on highway trucking corridors38. While Pelindo invested heavily in highway access infrastructure—most notably completing the 34.8-kilometer Cibitung-Cilincing Toll Road (JTCC) through its logistics sub-holding—direct intermodal rail freight connections to container berths remain underutilized or physically disconnected across most major commercial terminals11.  
This reliance on road drayage leaves the supply chain vulnerable to road congestion, pavement wear, vehicular emissions, and gate access delays3. While digital platforms like the Single Truck Identification Data (STID) and the Terminal Booking System (TBS) improved gate throughput, trucks continue to encounter traffic bottlenecks on municipal roads surrounding major ports37.  
Furthermore, industrial shippers often bypass specialized port-connected toll corridors due to separate toll fee structures, instead utilizing already congested public arterial highways11. Until Pelindo establishes integrated on-dock intermodal rail yards and coordinates single-tariff freight corridors with transportation agencies, efficiency gains achieved at the berth will continue to be offset by landside transport delays3.

### **Challenge 2: Competitive Cargo Dispersion and Cannibalization from Patimban Deep-Sea Port**

Constructed as a National Strategic Project in Subang, West Java, Patimban Deep-Sea Port is planned for an ultimate operational capacity of 7.5 million TEUs alongside a 600,000-vehicle terminal69. In 2024, Patimban's automobile terminal handled 200,631 export and import vehicles, reaching 82.3% of its Phase 1-1 design capacity69. In July 2026, Patimban opened international container operations with scheduled regular calls by the Mediterranean Shipping Company (MSC), serving automotive and manufacturing plants across the surrounding Rebana industrial corridor72.  
While national planners intended for Patimban to complement Tanjung Priok, it increasingly operates as a direct commercial competitor for high-margin cargo originating in the eastern industrial belt of West Java68. Pelindo does not hold an operating majority in Patimban’s container terminal, which is managed by a private joint venture between Japanese and domestic operators1.  
As international shipping lines add direct calls at Patimban to avoid Jakarta's urban gridlock, cargo is drawn away from Pelindo's terminals at Tanjung Priok68. This dynamic risks cannibalizing Tanjung Priok’s established traffic base and duplicating port capacity within a 120-kilometer radius, without necessarily expanding net national maritime trade24.

### **Challenge 3: Capital Allocation Pressures and Sovereign Dividend Mandates under Danantara**

Pelindo is currently funding several large-scale capital projects simultaneously: the offshore expansion of Kalibaru Terminal (New Priok Container Terminals 2 and 3), the elevated New Priok Eastern Access (NPEA) road corridor, ongoing berth expansion at Makassar New Port, and continuous navigation channel dredging at Kijing Terminal44.  
The group's long-term liabilities remain elevated at roughly Rp 53 to Rp 55 trillion, maintaining an adjusted net debt-to-EBITDA ratio of 3.5x5. At the same time, Pelindo paid Rp 1.0 trillion in state dividends for FY 2023 and Rp 1.07 trillion for FY 2024, facing expectations from BPI Danantara for higher annual dividend contributions from its high-performing SOEs15.  
These competing financial demands create tension in Pelindo's balance sheet management18. Major port infrastructure projects require substantial, patient capital with long payback horizons, while Danantara’s dividend targets pull cash away from retained earnings5.  
To fund its capital programs while meeting dividend expectations, Pelindo has turned to balance-sheet divestments, such as initiating the sale of a 65% equity stake in the Cibitung-Cilincing Toll Road concession to raise an estimated Rp 8 trillion for debt reduction38. Relying on asset sales to fund capital projects introduces balance sheet exposure, especially if regional trade volatility or foreign exchange swings affect debt service on the company's US-dollar obligations5.

## **Strategic Implications for Tanjung Priok Port**

As Indonesia’s primary commercial gateway handling more than half of the country's containerized foreign trade, Tanjung Priok serves as an operational bellwether for the entire Pelindo group2. The three strategic challenges discussed above have direct operational, capacity, and financial implications for the port24.  
Tanjung Priok's long-term expansion depends on the offshore Kalibaru development24. New Priok Container Terminal 1 (NPCT1) has operated since 2016 with a design capacity of 1.5 million TEUs24. Pelindo has advanced reclamation, soil stabilization, and civil works on NPCT 2 and NPCT 3 to bring Kalibaru’s total container handling capacity to 4.5 million TEUs, which will expand Tanjung Priok’s aggregate capacity beyond 11.5 million TEUs73.  
These Kalibaru berths offer deep drafts of \-16.0 meters LWS, allowing them to accommodate 15,000+ TEU ultra-large container vessels24. However, bringing this additional capacity online while cargo is actively being diverted to Patimban poses utilization risks72. Pelindo must carefully phase capital expenditure for NPCT 2 and NPCT 3 to avoid installing expensive quayside container cranes ahead of demand, matching berth expansion to actual volume growth in West Java20.  
Hinterland cargo connectivity represents another immediate operational hurdle11. Between 60% and 70% of cargo moving through Tanjung Priok originates from or is destined for the industrial corridors of Cikarang, Karawang, and Cibitung east of Jakarta38. To bypass severe bottlenecks on the Jakarta-Cikampek and Jakarta Intra-Urban toll roads, Pelindo launched the New Priok Eastern Access (NPEA) project68. Divided into distinct construction packages handled by state-owned contractors PTPP, Hutama Karya, and Brantas Abipraya, this 3.8-kilometer dedicated, elevated freight flyover links the eastern side of Kalibaru directly into the northern terminus of the Cibitung-Cilincing Toll Road (JTCC)74.  
Tanjung Priok’s competitive position relies heavily on the timely completion and pricing structure of this corridor11. Without an integrated toll system connecting the JTCC, the elevated MBZ corridor, and the NPEA flyover, drayage trucking fleets will continue using older public roads to avoid paying multiple tolls11. This would perpetuate chronic congestion around the port's gates, eroding the quayside handling improvements enabled by TOS Nusantara and the Terminal Booking System37.  
The commercial ramp-up of Patimban Port presents competitive challenges for Tanjung Priok's revenue mix69. Historically, Tanjung Priok handled nearly all international completely built-up (CBU) vehicle shipments through IPCC’s dedicated facility30. However, Patimban's vehicle terminal handled 200,631 units in 2024, handling substantial output from automotive manufacturing plants located nearby in Karawang and Subang69.  
This trend expanded into containerized shipping when Mediterranean Shipping Company (MSC) launched regular international container services at Patimban in July 2026, handling auto parts and general industrial cargo directly in West Java72.  
This diversion strips Tanjung Priok of high-margin automotive handling revenue, prompting SPMT and IPCC to adapt older quayside yards for domestic Ro-Ro shipping, construction equipment, and breakbulk operations30. If international shipping lines continue adding direct calls at Patimban, Tanjung Priok risks losing its dominant position as West Java’s sole export gateway, shifting its commercial focus toward domestic transshipment and imports serving the Greater Jakarta metropolitan area2.  
Quayside dwell times at Tanjung Priok have dropped substantially, falling from five to seven days a decade ago to an average of 2.67 days in late 2024 and mid-2025 across JICT, TPK Koja, and IPC TPK63. This improvement was achieved through customs digitalization, 24/7 scanning, and electronic pre-clearance procedures25.  
However, overall logistics performance remains limited by delays beyond the terminal gate63. Physical inspections, container depot processing, and customs hold-ups outside the port continue to generate transport bottlenecks63.  
Unless Pelindo coordinates with customs authorities to integrate inland container depots (ICDs) and dry ports into a single pre-clearance customs framework, efficiency gains inside terminal yards will continue to be offset by road delays across the Jakarta metropolitan transit network3.

## **Executive Brief**

By the third quarter of 2026, PT Pelabuhan Indonesia (Persero) completed its post-merger operational consolidation, establishing a standardized corporate structure across 71 commercial ports in 32 provinces1. Pelindo handles approximately 95% of Indonesia's commercial container traffic, processing 18.8 million TEUs in 2024 and pacing toward 20.2 to 20.5 million TEUs for full-year 20261.  
Operations are structured across four specialized sub-holdings: PT Pelindo Terminal Petikemas (container terminals), PT Pelindo Multi Terminal (bulk, general cargo, and automotive handling), PT Pelindo Jasa Maritim (marine support and equipment maintenance), and the newly rebranded PT Pelindo Sinergi Lokaseva (port-centric logistics, hinterland industrial parks, and freight tollways)2.  
Digital systems—notably TOS Nusantara for container terminals, PTOS-M for multipurpose cargo, and Phinisi for marine vessel dispatch—are deployed nationwide, cutting average vessel turnarounds across primary hubs6. On the sustainability front, the enterprise electrified quayside handling equipment and installed over 27 MVA of shore power capacity with PLN, aligned with the national Net Zero Emission 2060 roadmap34.  
Pelindo maintains a stable financial position, holding a 'BBB' (Stable) credit rating from Fitch Ratings with an adjusted net debt-to-EBITDA ratio of 3.5x and EBITDA margins of roughly 30%20. However, corporate governance changed significantly in early 2026 following BPI Danantara’s appointment of Achmad Muchtasyar as President Director, replacing Arif Suhartono13.  
Under Danantara’s oversight, management faces three core strategic challenges: heavy reliance on highway drayage (\>85%) that limits quayside velocity; cargo diversion to Patimban Deep-Sea Port, which handled over 200,000 vehicles in 2024 and opened regular international MSC container calls in July 2026; and capital allocation pressures from simultaneous mega-project spending and higher dividend demands18.  
For Tanjung Priok, these challenges affect operational and commercial planning24. Expanding Kalibaru through NPCT 2 and NPCT 3 will add valuable container capacity, but achieving high terminal utilization depends on completing the elevated New Priok Eastern Access (NPEA) road and establishing integrated toll rates with the Cibitung-Cilincing corridor11.  
At the same time, Patimban's rapid growth is capturing high-margin automotive volumes and export cargo from industrial manufacturing centers in West Java69. To preserve its market position and protect long-term capital returns, Pelindo must develop Tanjung Priok into an integrated multimodal logistics hub supported by streamlined intermodal corridors and coordinated customs clearance systems11.
