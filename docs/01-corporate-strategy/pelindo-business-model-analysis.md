# **Comprehensive Business Model and Value-Chain Analysis of PT Pelabuhan Indonesia (Persero): Strategic Transformation, Operational Architecture, and the Tanjung Priok Maritime Ecosystem**

## **Executive Summary and Post-Merger Strategic Architecture**

The integration of Indonesia’s port sector on October 1, 2021—merging PT Pelabuhan Indonesia I, II, III, and IV into a single enterprise, PT Pelabuhan Indonesia (Persero) ("Pelindo")—fundamentally restructured the institutional landscape of Southeast Asian maritime trade1. Prior to this consolidation, the domestic port sector suffered from institutional fragmentation. Four state-owned port corporations operated as regionally bounded monopolies across separate geographic zones, creating incompatible terminal operating technologies, duplicate capital investment programs, misaligned marine tariff structures, and systemic equipment imbalances between the industrial center of Western Indonesia and the resource-rich but logistics-deprived outer islands.  
To overcome these structural handicaps, the post-merger corporate architecture transitioned Pelindo from a legacy administrative model to an integrated holding-and-subholding structure2. In this arrangement, the parent company retains sovereign port authority functions, portfolio capital allocation, and group-level digital governance, while commercial operations are concentrated into four specialized business clusters2:  
The first cluster, PT Pelindo Terminal Petikemas (SPTP), standardizes container terminal operations across the Indonesian archipelago3. SPTP governs deep-sea and coastal container handling, harmonizes Terminal Operating Systems (TOS), aligns quayside equipment investments, and manages key commercial joint ventures2.  
The second cluster, PT Pelindo Multi Terminal (SPMT), manages the commercial flow of non-containerized cargo3. This includes liquid bulk (principally crude palm oil and refined petroleum derivatives), dry bulk (coal, mineral ores, clinker, and grain), breakbulk project cargo, and automotive roll-on/roll-off (Ro-Ro) handling1.  
The third cluster, PT Pelindo Jasa Maritim (SPJM), manages the wet marine interface3. SPJM delivers commercial vessel pilotage, towage, berthing services, bunkering, marine engineering, and fairway maintenance dredging, deploying its maritime assets through operating subsidiaries such as the publicly listed PT Jasa Armada Indonesia Tbk (IPCM)1.  
The fourth cluster, PT Pelindo Solusi Logistik (SPSL), operates as the multimodal logistics and hinterland connectivity division3. SPSL develops dry ports, inland container depots, bonded logistics centers (Pusat Logistik Berikat), multimodal freight-forwarding corridors, and direct-access port toll road networks, serving as the physical bridge between deep-water berths and hinterland industrial clusters9.  
This functional specialization has generated documented capital expenditure and operational efficiencies. In its initial operating year post-merger, Pelindo secured IDR 1.3 trillion in recurring operational efficiencies and commercial optimizations12. Consolidated treasury management enabled a large-scale deleveraging campaign, reducing long-term debt liabilities from IDR 50.90 trillion at the point of merger in 2021 to IDR 49.87 trillion in 2023, with debt trending toward IDR 41.93 trillion14. Across the multi-year post-merger integration horizon, total group debt retirements reached between IDR 11.00 trillion and IDR 12.75 trillion, yielding roughly IDR 750 billion in recurring annual cost-of-fund savings that have been reallocated toward equipment electrification and fairway expansions1.  
Financially, Pelindo registered an audited consolidated net profit of IDR 4.01 trillion in 2023, while sustaining earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation, and amortization (EBITDA) of IDR 10.35 trillion, up from IDR 10.11 trillion in 202215. Operating revenue expanded consistently, climbing to IDR 35.48 trillion in 202519. Concurrently, operational volume expanded across all primary business activities: group container throughput reached 17.7 million twenty-foot equivalent units (TEUs) in 2023 and surpassed 18.1 million TEUs thereafter, non-container dry and liquid cargo volumes reached 170 million metric tons, and vessel traffic reached 303,221 ship calls encompassing 1.28 billion gross tonnage (GT)15.

## **End-to-End Business Model Canvas Analysis**

### **Customer Segments and Customer Relationships**

Pelindo serves a layered, interdependent customer base that spans international maritime shipping lines, domestic feeder operators, beneficial cargo owners (BCOs), third-party logistics providers (3PLs), and industrial land developers. The first customer segment comprises global mainline ocean carriers operating within global vessel-sharing consortia, such as the 2M Alliance, Ocean Alliance, and Premier Alliance, alongside non-aligned global carriers. These shipping conglomerates deploy ultra-large container vessels requiring deep-water drafts (-14 to \-16 meters LWS), guaranteed berthing windows, and high gross crane productivity rates exceeding 25 to 30 moves per crane per hour16.  
The second segment consists of domestic shipping operators governed by Indonesia's statutory cabotage mandate. Lines such as Meratus, Samudera Indonesia, Temas Line, and Tanto Intim Line operate coastal feeder vessels linking central distribution transshipment gateways to secondary and tertiary island ports. The third segment comprises large beneficial cargo owners, industrial manufacturers, and state-owned commercial entities, such as PT Pertamina, PT Semen Indonesia, and PT Pupuk Indonesia, moving raw dry bulk, energy fuels, and agricultural commodities26. The remaining customer tiers consist of licensed freight forwarders affiliated with the Indonesian Logistics and Forwarders Association (ALFI), intermodal drayage carriers, and corporate tenants leasing waterfront land within integrated special economic zones such as the Java Integrated Industrial and Port Estate (JIIPE) at Manyar, Gresik20.  
Customer relationships are maintained through contractual service-level agreements (SLAs), institutional joint-venture boards, and automated transactional touchpoints. For tier-one global shipping alliances, Pelindo executes bilateral berthing contracts that tie volume discounts to minimum annual throughput thresholds and establish financial penalties for excessive berth waiting times. For industrial cargo owners and domestic lines, Pelindo deploys unified key account management programs across its subholdings, allowing customers to negotiate integrated pilotage, terminal handling, and hinterland staging agreements via a single commercial interface. Transactional customer interactions are largely automated; web-based portals and customer service desks handle standard bookings, while joint venture governance boards manage long-term strategic relationships with terminal operating partners1.

### **Value Propositions and Distribution Channels**

Pelindo’s commercial value propositions focus on reducing vessel turn-around times (TRT), providing end-to-end multimodal logistics visibility, standardizing national port tariffs, and delivering automated clearance workflows2. For ocean carriers, Pelindo reduces unproductive anchorage waiting time through integrated marine dispatching, allowing shipping lines to maintain tight international sailing rotations and avoid burning auxiliary fuel2. For beneficial cargo owners and industrial shippers, Pelindo reduces supply chain volatility by integrating quayside terminal gates directly with designated expressway corridors, eliminating intermediate drayage stops and avoiding local urban road bottlenecks9. Across regional hubs, Pelindo standardizes handling procedures, providing uniform operational efficiency whether a ship calls at Tanjung Priok in Java or regional facilities in Belawan or Ambon16.  
These value propositions are delivered through physical port channels and digital application suites. Physical interaction occurs at Pelindo’s quayside facilities, container yards, multipurpose open storage areas, bonded logistics centers, and dedicated access highways, most notably the Jalan Tol Cibitung-Cilincing (JTCC) corridor9. Digital delivery is coordinated through cloud platforms: the Ministry of Transportation's Inaportnet single-window clearing system interfaces with Pelindo's Phinnisi marine scheduling engine, terminal operating systems (TOS and PTOS-M), and the Integrated Billing System (IBS)2. The Integrated Billing System centralizes documentation, invoicing, and cashless e-banking settlements across all terminals, allowing shipping lines and logistics providers to process delivery orders, settle terminal charges, and clear cargo releases without visiting physical billing counters20.

### **Key Activities and Critical Resources**

Pelindo’s operations span four interconnected maritime activities: marine vessel maneuvering, quayside stevedoring, container yard management, and landside hinterland distribution. Marine operations encompass vessel traffic monitoring, licensed piloting through narrow navigational fairways, harbor tug maneuvering, and quayside berthing7. Quayside stevedoring involves discharging and loading containers, dry bulk commodities, and wheeled vehicles using heavy dockside cranes. Container yard and warehouse operations include cargo stacking, reefer temperature monitoring, empty-container sorting, customs physical examination staging, and security screening4. Hinterland logistics activities involve operating bonded container depots, coordinating freight transfers with national rail links, and managing dedicated expressways9.  
These operations rely on extensive capital assets, strategic real estate, and proprietary software systems. Pelindo’s physical capital includes deep-water commercial harbors, dredged navigational channels, breakwaters, and quayside berths capable of accommodating post-Panamax and neo-Panamax vessels25. Quayside equipment comprises Super Post-Panamax twin-lift Ship-to-Shore (STS) container cranes, rail-mounted gantry cranes, electric rubber-tyred gantry (e-RTG) cranes, reachstackers, and specialized bulk conveyances34. Marine assets include an operational fleet of over 56 tugboats, 26 pilot boats, and 5 mooring boats managed by SPJM and its publicly traded operating arm, PT Jasa Armada Indonesia Tbk (IPCM)35. Digital assets comprise proprietary software frameworks, real-time yard optimization algorithms, optical character recognition gate access modules, and the Single Truck Identification (STID) vehicle database2.

### **Key Partners and Stakeholder Ecosystem**

Pelindo relies on a diverse network of government regulators, multinational terminal operators, intermodal transport companies, and organized labor unions. The primary regulatory authority is the Ministry of Transportation, acting through the Directorate General of Sea Transportation (Ditjen Hubla) and local Port Authority offices (Kantor Kesyahbandaran dan Otoritas Pelabuhan \- KSOP), which oversee maritime safety, navigation rules, and statutory port concession frameworks37. Sovereign governance and shareholder oversight are directed by the Ministry of State-Owned Enterprises and the sovereign asset management agency BP Danantara32. Fiscal border control is managed by the Directorate General of Customs and Excise (DJBC) under the Ministry of Finance, which enforces clearance protocols through the Indonesia National Single Window (INSW) platform31.  
Pelindo maintains equity joint ventures with global terminal operators to access international container volumes and modern port practices. Key partners include Hutchison Port Holdings at the Jakarta International Container Terminal (JICT), alongside Mitsui & Co., NYK Line, and PSA International at New Priok Container Terminal One (NPCT1)5. On the landside, Pelindo interfaces with PT Kereta Api Indonesia (Persero) and its freight subsidiary PT KAI Logistik to operate intermodal rail terminals, as well as the Indonesian Trucking Association (Aptrindo) to coordinate road transport32. Quayside cargo handling relies on organized dockworker cooperatives (Tenaga Kerja Bongkar Muat \- TKBM), which supply registered labor teams under statutory collective bargaining agreements.

### **Cost Drivers and Revenue Streams**

Pelindo's cost baseline is asset-heavy and capital-intensive, driven by fuel consumption, mechanical maintenance, labor overhead, concession royalties, and asset depreciation. Marine operations require substantial marine gas oil (MGO) consumption to power harbor tugs, alongside recurring capital outlays for regulatory slipway drydockings, hull surveys, and engine maintenance across the IPCM tug fleet8. Terminal operations incur significant utility expenses, particularly electricity tariffs for STS cranes, e-RTGs, and refrigerated container plug-in banks4. Cost drivers also include negotiated labor payments to TKBM dockworker unions, maintenance dredging to remove silt from approach channels, depreciation expenses on marine civil infrastructure, and statutory non-tax state revenue (PNBP) concession fees remitted to the national government1.  
Pelindo monetizes services through regulated port dues, handling tariffs, logistics fees, and property leases41. Marine revenues include anchor dues (jasa labuh) billed against vessel gross tonnage, berth dues (jasa tambat) charged on gross tonnage and mooring duration, and regulated pilotage and towage tariffs42. Cargo handling generates Container Handling Charges (CHC) and Terminal Handling Charges (THC) for quayside crane lifts, lift-on/lift-off (Lo-Lo) fees for yard transfers, and stevedoring fees for non-containerized bulk commodities41. Landside earnings include progressive container yard storage surcharges for boxes that exceed statutory free dwell periods, toll collections from the Jalan Tol Cibitung-Cilincing via PT CTP Tollways, bonded warehouse square-meter leases, and industrial waterfront land concessions9.

## **End-to-End Maritime Value Chain at Tanjung Priok**

Port of Tanjung Priok in Jakarta serves as the core maritime gateway of the Indonesian economy, processing over 60% of the nation’s international containerized trade. The port's end-to-end maritime value chain encompasses five sequential operational stages: marine arrival, quayside stevedoring, container yard staging, customs clearance, and landside hinterland transport.  
The value chain begins when an incoming vessel enters the Tanjung Priok Outer Anchorage. Vessel traffic control operators log the ship's arrival through the Phinnisi digital platform, cross-referencing inbound cargo manifests with quay availability and the master vessel berthing plan2. Once clearance is granted by the harbor master (KSOP), marine operations are executed by SPJM through its specialized vessel operating arm, PT Jasa Armada Indonesia Tbk (IPCM)8. A licensed harbor pilot boards the vessel via an IPCM pilot launch, while two to four Azimuth Stern Drive (ASD) harbor tugboats, each providing 3,200 to 5,000 brake horsepower, meet the vessel to assist navigation through the inner fairway35.  
Navigational access is governed by fairway draft constraints: legacy inner basins maintain channel depths between \-8.5 and \-12.0 meters LWS, limiting access to smaller feeder ships, whereas the outer New Priok Kalibaru channel provides a depth of \-16.0 meters LWS, enabling direct access for fully laden neo-Panamax container vessels25. Marine operations generate consistent earnings for Pelindo; IPCM recorded full-year 2023 revenues of IDR 1.1 trillion with net profits of IDR 157.6 billion, expanding its nine-month 2024 revenue to IDR 946.62 billion, with over 85% of total operating income generated by vessel towing operations42.  
Once moored, quayside container stevedoring operations commence under SPTP management. International container handling is distributed across three primary joint-venture terminals and one wholly owned operating subsidiary:  
The Jakarta International Container Terminal (JICT), a joint venture between Pelindo (49%) and Hutchison Port Holdings (51%), remains the largest facility in Tanjung Priok, operating 1,640 meters of continuous linear berth dredged to \-14.0 meters LWS25. During the first nine months of 2025, JICT processed 1,609,253 TEUs, capturing a 39.5% share of total port container throughput5.  
New Priok Container Terminal One (NPCT1), located within the Kalibaru offshore expansion zone, is operated by a joint venture between Pelindo, Mitsui & Co., NYK Line, and PSA International5. NPCT1 operates an 850-meter wharf dredged to \-16.0 meters LWS, equipped with Super Post-Panamax dual-hoist STS cranes25. NPCT1 handled 1,155,159 TEUs over the first nine months of 2025 (a 28.3% port share), expanding its volume by 19.6% year-on-year as international shipping consortia shifted long-haul transpacific and direct European services to its deeper berths5.  
Terminal Petikemas Koja (TPK Koja) operates under a revenue-sharing joint operation (KSO) between Pelindo and PT Ocean Terminal Petikemas, providing 650 meters of linear berth at a \-14.0 meter LWS depth and handling roughly 18% of port throughput33. TPK Koja completed an IDR 803 billion equipment modernization program that replaced older Panamax cranes with Super Post-Panamax units, raising total handling capacity to 1.29 million TEUs per year34.  
The remaining international and domestic container traffic is handled by IPC Terminal Petikemas (IPC TPK), an operating subsidiary that handled 305,880 TEUs over the first nine months of 2025, up 21.0% year-on-year5. Across these terminals, consolidated international throughput at Tanjung Priok reached 4,075,521 TEUs through the third quarter of 2025, on track to match or exceed the 7.60 million to 8.30 million TEU full-year volumes recorded across 2024 and 20255.

From the quayside, STS cranes transfer containers directly onto terminal chassis trucks, which haul boxes into designated container yard (CY) blocks where electric rubber-tyred gantry (e-RTG) cranes stack them up to five containers high. Yard dwell time (![][image1]) remains the key metric of landside operational efficiency, measuring the elapsed time between container discharge from a ship and gate exit31. Dwell time comprises three distinct administrative stages:  
![][image2]  
The pre-clearance phase (![][image3]) spans the period from container unloading onto the yard to the importer’s formal submission of the import declaration (Pemberitahuan Impor Barang \- PIB) to customs authorities31. This stage represents the largest delay in the port cycle, averaging 1.50 to 1.80 days and accounting for over 50% of total container dwell time31. Delays stem primarily from regulatory hurdles, as importers must obtain non-tariff import approvals (Larangan dan Pembatasan \- Lartas) from technical ministries before filing documentation31.  
The customs clearance phase (![][image4]) begins once the PIB is lodged within the Indonesia National Single Window (INSW)31. Customs systems automatically route entries based on importer compliance profiles: green-channel cargo (roughly 80% of volume) clears via automated electronic import release permits (Surat Persetujuan Pengeluaran Barang \- SPPB) in 0.30 to 0.50 days; yellow-channel shipments undergo manual document audits; and red-channel shipments require physical container destuffing and scanner inspections at dedicated staging facilities31.  
The post-clearance phase (![][image5]) extends from SPPB issuance to physical gate exit31. During this window, the cargo owner settles terminal handling and storage fees through the Integrated Billing System, secures an electronic delivery order from the ocean carrier, and dispatches a drayage truck to haul the container out of the port20. This final phase averages 0.90 to 1.10 days31. Through terminal digitization and cross-agency integration, total container dwell time at Tanjung Priok has fallen from 7.0 to 8.0 days down to 2.80 to 3.20 days, meeting the national target of under 3.0 days31.

The final stage of the value chain connects terminal gates to hinterland manufacturing corridors. Gate throughput is coordinated by the Single Truck Identification (STID) framework, which has enrolled over 7,451 licensed drayage trucks with RFID and optical character recognition credentials36. By verifying booking credentials and driver identification at terminal smart gates, STID has reduced internal Truck Roundabout Times (TRT) to under 15 minutes per transaction20.  
To move cargo into industrial centers, Pelindo's logistics subholding (SPSL) developed the 34-kilometer Jalan Tol Cibitung-Cilincing (JTCC) expressway via its subsidiary PT Cibitung Tanjung Priok Port Tollways9. Representing an infrastructure investment of IDR 9.0 trillion, the JTCC provides a direct freight link connecting terminal gates to manufacturing clusters in Cikarang and Karawang, bypassing congested municipal roadways in Northern Jakarta14. Under a capital recycling strategy, Pelindo has pursued a 65% equity divestment of the tollway to return capital to its balance sheet while preserving dedicated access through the New Priok Eastern Access (NPEA) road project14.  
Complementing highway transport, the port connects to the Cikarang Dry Port (CDP) inland container terminal and operates a direct rail freight connection linking JICT to the national railway network via PT KAI Logistik32. However, intermodal rail accounts for less than 5% of hinterland container traffic due to the cost competitiveness of road transport over short distances (40 to 60 kilometers) and additional lift-on/lift-off charges incurred at rail sidings.

## **Macroeconomic Value Creation and National Logistics Footprint**

Pelindo's port infrastructure plays a central role in shaping national economic efficiency and inter-island connectivity. Across the Indonesian archipelago, maritime transport efficiency directly influences domestic trade competitiveness, regional price parity, and global export performance56.  
Historically, high logistics costs have placed a structural burden on the Indonesian economy. In 2018, data from the Ministry of National Development Planning (Bappenas) indicated that national logistics costs equaled 23.80% of Gross Domestic Product (GDP), significantly higher than the ASEAN regional average44. By September 2023, cross-agency reforms and port modernization reduced national logistics costs to 14.29% of GDP, with government planning targets aiming for 8.00% of GDP by 204544.  
Pelindo contributed to this efficiency gain by standardizing operating practices across primary and secondary ports. Prior to the 2021 merger, regional terminals outside Java operated with aging handling equipment and uncoordinated berthing schedules, leading to low crane productivity and protracted vessel stays16. Post-merger operational standardization raised Berth Moves per Hour (BSH) at regional terminals—such as Belawan in North Sumatra and Ambon in Maluku—from historical averages of 12 to 20 moves up to 35 to 45 gross moves per hour, cutting commercial ship port stays in half16. This increased productivity lowered operating costs for domestic shipping lines, helping narrow inter-island retail price disparities under the national maritime highway ("Tol Laut") framework32.  
Pelindo also serves as a source of non-tax revenue and fiscal dividends for the national government. In 2021 and 2022, Pelindo contributed IDR 6.03 trillion to state coffers through corporate income taxes, value-added taxes, concession royalties, and shareholder dividends40. Annual remittances grew to IDR 7.81 trillion in 2024, on track to meet cumulative state revenue targets of IDR 21.00 trillion as operational performance matures under the sovereign oversight of BP Danantara15.  
Beyond direct fiscal contributions, port operations generate value across the industrial economy. Deepened channel depths at NPCT1 (-16.0 meters LWS) allow international carriers to run direct mainline calls to the United States and Europe without intermediate transshipment in Singapore, reducing ocean freight costs for domestic exporters and cutting transit times by several days25. In West Java's manufacturing belt, reduced port dwell times and direct expressway access through the JTCC have lowered buffer inventory requirements for automotive and electronics manufacturers, improving supply chain integration for foreign direct investment9.

## **Value Leakage Points, Structural Bottlenecks, and Inefficiencies**

Despite documented operational improvements following the 2021 merger, Pelindo’s maritime and hinterland value chain continues to experience value leakage across several key interfaces:  
The primary bottleneck in the terminal cycle remains administrative pre-clearance delays. While customs processing and quayside handling operate efficiently, pre-clearance continues to account for over half of total container dwell time31. This delay stems from uncoordinated import licensing requirements (Lartas) administered by more than 15 separate government ministries and agencies, including the Ministry of Trade, Ministry of Industry, and the Food and Drug Authority31. Because importers often fail to secure required surveyor reports, technical standardizations, and import quotas prior to ship arrival, containers sit on container yards before import declarations (PIB) can be lodged, driving up demurrage costs and consuming terminal space31.  
A second source of value leakage is the low utilization of intermodal freight rail. Over 95% of containerized freight moving to and from Tanjung Priok relies on highway trucking, causing chronic congestion across North Jakarta’s road network. Although JICT operates an active dockside rail terminal linked to PT KAI Logistik, rail carries under 5% of container volume32. This imbalance persists because short-haul rail trips to the Cikarang industrial zone incur double handling charges—specifically, lift-on/lift-off fees at both the dockside rail spur and inland depots—which erase rail's fuel cost advantages relative to point-to-point road drayage.  
A third operational challenge involves empty-container repositioning. Marine container terminals and off-dock empty depots along the Cakung-Cilincing corridor operate without shared dispatching platforms. Drayage trucks discharging laden import boxes at factories frequently return empty to the port, rather than reloading empty containers for export dispatch. This lack of freight matching generates thousands of empty-chassis truck trips, exacerbating local highway congestion, increasing diesel emissions, and raising transport costs per TEU.  
A fourth inefficiency arises from fragmented terminal operating software across Tanjung Priok’s individual container facilities. JICT, NPCT1, and TPK Koja utilize separate terminal software platforms, reflecting their distinct historical joint-venture partnerships5. Because these software suites are not unified under a single operating layer, yard space, berth assignments, and quayside cranes cannot be dynamically shared across terminals during arrival surges5. When one terminal approaches capacity, incoming ships cannot be reassigned to adjacent berths without incurring new pilot bookings, inter-terminal drayage charges, and administrative customs revisions.  
Finally, inter-island trade imbalances present persistent operational challenges under statutory cabotage rules. Because domestic trade flows outward from Java to consumers in outer islands, container ships returning to Tanjung Priok from Eastern Indonesia frequently sail with low load factors or return empty containers. These empty boxes accumulate in container yards across Tanjung Priok and Tanjung Perak, taking up storage capacity and increasing container yard re-handling costs.

## **Strategic Improvement Roadmaps and Digital Interventions**

To resolve operational bottlenecks and build an integrated gateway ecosystem, Pelindo is pursuing four capital and technological modernization initiatives:  
The first priority is digital harmonization across its core operating clusters. Pelindo is rolling out its proprietary Phinnisi vessel dispatch system and the unified Pelindo Terminal Operating System Multipurpose (PTOS-M) across all commercial branches2. This digital integration will allow marine control centers to monitor tugboat movements, automate pilot assignments, and dynamically track vessel waiting times, targeting marine dispatch response times of under 15 minutes2. Concurrently, deploying PTOS-M creates a shared data platform across container terminals, enabling dynamic berth and crane sharing to absorb arrival surges without operational delays2.  
The second priority focuses on hinterland highway access and intermodal rail improvements. Pelindo is constructing the New Priok Eastern Access (NPEA) road project to connect New Priok Kalibaru berths directly to the Cibitung-Cilincing expressway network14. This elevated road corridor will divert heavy truck traffic off urban surface streets, providing manufacturing plants in Cikarang and Karawang with an unimpeded freight path to deep-water quays9. In parallel, Pelindo is working with PT KAI Logistik to restructure intermodal rail fees, exploring targeted fee reductions for dockside rail lift-on/lift-off handling to encourage container traffic off highway networks and onto freight rail32.  
The third initiative centers on capital recycling and balance sheet optimization. Pelindo has advanced plans to divest a 65% equity stake in the Jalan Tol Cibitung-Cilincing (JTCC) concession, which is valued at roughly IDR 9.0 trillion14. This transaction will allow Pelindo to recycle infrastructure capital, reduce group debt toward IDR 41.93 trillion, and redeploy equity proceeds to fund the construction of Kalibaru Phase 2 container berths and logistics parks14.  
The fourth priority targets landside container coordination through integrated empty-depot networks. SPSL is developing shared container depots linked directly to the Single Truck ID system36. By integrating depot inventories with ocean carrier bookings, drayage operators will be able to pick up export boxes directly after delivering import cargo, eliminating empty-chassis truck trips and reducing traffic density along primary access roads.

## **Business Model Section for the K36 Immersion Canvas**

### **Systemic Mandate and Strategic Architecture**

Pelindo operates as Indonesia’s unified commercial port authority and terminal operator, managing maritime gateway infrastructure, facilitating archipelagic trade, and supporting the reduction of national logistics costs from 14.29% toward 8.00% of GDP44. The group operates under a centralized holding model governing four specialized subholdings: PT Pelindo Terminal Petikemas (container terminals), PT Pelindo Multi Terminal (bulk and breakbulk cargo), PT Pelindo Jasa Maritim (marine vessel services), and PT Pelindo Solusi Logistik (hinterland logistics and expressways)3.

### **Capability Stack and Resource Base**

* *Marine Operations Fleet:* Exclusive management of commercial navigation fairways, alongside an active fleet of 87+ tug, pilot, and mooring vessels managed by SPJM and PT Jasa Armada Indonesia Tbk (IPCM)35.  
* *Deep-Water Terminal Superstructure:* Over 4,500 meters of continuous container wharves at Tanjung Priok, dredged to alongside depths of up to \-16.0 meters LWS and equipped with Super Post-Panamax dual-hoist STS cranes25.  
* *Integrated Digital Platforms:* Proprietary software systems, including Phinnisi (marine vessel dispatch), PTOS-M (multipurpose and container yard management), the Integrated Billing System, and Single Truck ID (STID)2.  
* *Hinterland Transport Corridors:* Dedicated freight infrastructure, including the 34-kilometer Jalan Tol Cibitung-Cilincing (JTCC) and the New Priok Eastern Access corridor9.

### **Strategic Moats and Competitive Defensibility**

* *Statutory Concession Mandate:* Exclusive operating authority as the primary Port Business Entity (Badan Usaha Pelabuhan) across major commercial ports under long-term state concession agreements.  
* *High Capital Barriers to Entry:* Deep-water fairways, offshore breakwaters, and heavy quayside crane installations represent multi-trillion-rupiah investments that protect primary hub ports from direct commercial replication14.  
* *Captive Marine Pilotage and Towage Rights:* Statutory authority over marine services across commercial port basins, providing recurring, high-margin revenue through SPJM and IPCM8.  
* *Global Terminal Operator Partnerships:* Joint-venture ownership arrangements with Hutchison Ports, Mitsui & Co., NYK Line, and PSA International that secure international shipping line calls and integrate global terminal practices5.

### **Monetization and Revenue Architecture**

* *Marine Navigation Dues:* Vessel anchor charges, berthing fees, and pilotage/towage tariffs billed against gross tonnage and movement duration42.  
* *Quayside Terminal Handling:* Regulated Container Handling Charges (CHC/THC) and lift-on/lift-off (Lo-Lo) tariffs billed to ocean carriers and beneficial cargo owners41.  
* *Container Yard Storage Tariffs:* Progressive storage surcharges levied on import containers resting beyond standard free-time periods31.  
* *Logistics Infrastructure and Property:* Toll collections from the JTCC expressway corridor, bonded logistics warehouse leases, and land concessions across port-adjacent industrial zones9.

### **Strategic Execution Risks**

* *Regulatory Pre-Clearance Exposure:* Port dwell times remain vulnerable to delays in trade permit processing across non-transport ministries31.  
* *Road Drayage Congestion:* Over-reliance on highway trucking leaves terminal gate velocity exposed to municipal traffic bottlenecks along arterial corridors.  
* *Disparate Terminal Systems:* The presence of separate terminal operating software across joint-venture terminals restricts flexible quayside crane and berth sharing2.

### **Prescriptive Strategic Interventions**

> 1. Complete the planned 65% equity divestment of the JTCC tollway concession to recover IDR 8.0 to 9.0 trillion in invested capital, deleverage group balance-sheet liabilities, and fund the construction of Kalibaru Phase 2 container berths14.  
> 2. Standardize the TOS software environment across JICT, NPCT1, and TPK Koja through a unified PTOS-M operating layer, enabling shared quayside berthing allocations during peak arrival windows2.  
> 3. Partner with PT KAI Logistik to restructure intermodal rail lift charges, equalizing rail transport costs with highway trucking to shift industrial container volumes from road to rail32.  
> 4. Accelerate construction of the New Priok Eastern Access elevated highway to provide an uninterrupted freight link between deep-water berths and West Java's manufacturing corridors14.
