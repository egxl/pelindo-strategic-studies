# **Strategic Port Benchmarking and Operational Transformation Framework for Tanjung Priok and Pelindo**

## **Operational Baseline and Performance Diagnostics of Tanjung Priok**

### **Post-Merger Institutional Architecture and Governance Dynamics**

The legal consolidation of Pelindo I, II, III, and IV into PT Pelabuhan Indonesia (Persero) in October 2021 structurally unified state-owned port operations across the Indonesian archipelago into a single commercial holding enterprise1. Under this unified governance model, operational execution was reorganized into four functional subholdings: PT Pelindo Terminal Petikemas (SPTP) governing container terminals, PT Pelindo Multi Terminal (SPMT) managing non-containerized cargo, PT Pelindo Solusi Logistik (SPSL) steering inland logistics properties and hinterland connectivity, and PT Pelindo Jasa Maritim (SPJM) managing marine services, pilotage, tug operations, and utilities3.  
Within the Port of Tanjung Priok—which processes approximately half of Indonesia’s international containerized traffic6—governance remains bifurcated between sovereign maritime administration and commercial concession operations2. Statutory oversight, navigation safety, and regulatory supervision are vested in the Port Authority (Kantor Otoritas Pelabuhan Utama Tanjung Priok) under the Directorate General of Sea Transportation (DGST), Ministry of Transportation2. Terminal handling operations, conversely, are fragmented among distinct operating business units and joint-venture corporate entities2. These include Jakarta International Container Terminal (JICT, a joint venture between Pelindo and Hutchison Ports)2, TPK Koja, New Priok Container Terminal 1 (NPCT1, operated alongside Mitsui & Co., NYK Line, and PSA International)2, and IPC Terminal Petikemas (IPC TPK)8. This multi-operator configuration creates distinct commercial fiefdoms within a single contiguous harbor, resulting in operational misalignments across terminal boundaries.

### **Throughput Trajectory and International Performance Indexing**

Container traffic across Tanjung Priok expanded from 6.85 million twenty-foot equivalent units (TEUs) in 2022 to approximately 7.83 million TEUs in 2024, reaching 8.30 million TEUs in 20259. In the World Bank and S\&P Global Market Intelligence Container Port Performance Index (CPPI), Tanjung Priok experienced a notable ascent, moving from 281st position in the 2022 evaluation to 23rd globally in the 2023 index7.  
This recorded elevation reflects substantial reductions in median vessel turnaround times at the quay and modernized ship-to-shore crane productivity across dedicated deep-water facilities such as NPCT17. However, the vessel-centric methodology of the CPPI—which assesses performance based exclusively on container ship elapsed port hours—masks chronic landside inefficiencies, extreme drayage queue variability, and severe intermodal deficits that continue to impact Indonesian supply chains14.

### **Dwell Time Decomposition and Terminal Yard Mechanics**

Although aggregate import container dwell times have fallen from historical highs of 6.0 to 6.5 days to between 2.8 and 3.2 days12, stage-by-stage analysis demonstrates that administrative and commercial behavioral factors dominate physical handling delays23. Pre-customs clearance accounts for 50% to 65% of total yard dwell time (averaging 1.5 to 2.0 days), driven by delayed electronic import declaration (Pemberitahuan Impor Barang/PIB) submissions by cargo consignees, manifest processing latencies by ocean carriers, and non-tariff import permissions across ministerial licensing bodies18.  
Customs clearance itself requires 10% to 15% of dwell duration (0.3 to 0.5 days) for the overwhelming majority of boxes processed through the automated Green Channel by the Directorate General of Customs and Excise (DJBC), but surges substantially whenever Red Channel physical inspections or quarantine interventions are mandated23.  
Post-customs clearance consumes 25% to 35% of total dwell time (0.8 to 1.2 days), during which legally cleared boxes remain stored in prime terminal yards23. This occurs because consignees frequently leverage terminal storage tariffs as lower-cost alternative warehousing compared to private off-dock logistics facilities, compounding the impact of transport scheduling misalignments25.  
As terminal yard density exceeds 70% of static holding capacity, unproductive yard crane shuffling increases nonlinearly28. This yard congestion compromises quay crane cycle velocity and backs up drayage truck queues into surrounding urban access corridors16.

### **Hinterland Intermodal Bottlenecks and Drayage Economics**

Hinterland cargo movements between Tanjung Priok and the primary industrial manufacturing clusters of West Java—located in Cikarang, Karawang, and Cibitung—rely almost exclusively on road transport, which accounts for over 95% of total inland freight volume17. This short transit corridor of 50 to 60 kilometers is subject to severe road congestion, with heavy tractor-trailers frequently spending up to 75% of total trip duration idling in traffic18.  
Dedicated freight rail service between Tanjung Priok’s Pasoso railhead and the Cikarang Dry Port (CDP) facility operates at a negligible volume of approximately two train departures per day hauling 30 flatcars each21. Freight rail utilization is hindered by double-handling operational costs32.  
Because the national rail spur terminates at Pasoso yard outside the maritime terminal gates rather than directly on the quay, containers must be discharged from deep-sea vessels, loaded onto internal yard tractors, shuttled across public roads to Pasoso, lifted onto railcars, hauled to Cikarang, and handled again by inland reach stackers31.  
This intermediate transfer incurs an operational penalty of IDR 750,000 to IDR 1,500,000 per box, rendering short-haul rail freight economically uncompetitive with direct road trucking33. The alternative Cikarang Bekasi Laut (CBL) inland waterway canal project, planned to carry 4,000 daily container moves via inland barges, remains stalled due to land acquisition challenges, environmental dredging needs, and uncertain public-private financial models17.

### **Digital Systems Landscape and Interoperability Deficits**

Pelindo has advanced digital modernization programs across its operating branches, but Tanjung Priok continues to experience horizontal integration gaps among its administrative software platforms16. Marine dispatch, pilot assignment, and tug scheduling are coordinated through the *Phinnisi* vessel application1. Quayside and yard assets are split across proprietary software (such as Navis SPARCS at JICT and Cosmos at NPCT1) and Pelindo's corporate system, *TOS Nusantara* / *Pelindo Terminal Operating System Multipurpose (PTOS-M)*1. Drayage access is managed via Single Truck Identification Data (STID) and individual Terminal Truck Booking Systems (TBS)6.  
These digital layers operate in functional silos16. The STID registry confirms driver licensing and vehicle mechanical inspection compliance but does not feed dynamic truck-chassis tracking into terminal yard crane optimization engines6.  
Similarly, the National Logistics Ecosystem (NLE)—instituted to coordinate cross-ministerial trade documentation, customs, and private trucking dispatch—remains loosely connected to quayside crane assignments22. This lack of horizontal integration prevents dispatchers from pacing vessel arrivals, quayside unlashing, yard crane moves, and gate appointments into a synchronized logistics flow16.

### **Operational Excellence and Inter-Terminal Transfers**

Multi-terminal container gateways frequently experience friction when transshipment containers or intermodal consignments must move across adjacent, separately operated terminal leases48. In the Port of Busan, where multiple terminal operating companies manage parallel facilities across Busan New Port, Inter-Terminal Transfers (ITT) historically caused road congestion and extended gate processing queues57. Busan addressed this through its Chain Portal ITT platform48.  
The system uses a neutral coordination engine that monitors container availability and drayage truck positions across operating terminals48. Instead of treating each inter-terminal movement as a distinct gate-out and gate-in transaction, the platform clusters transfer requests and assigns them to empty return trucks moving between adjacent facilities48.  
This fleet coordination reduced empty truck journeys by 30% and cut inter-terminal turnaround times by 25%48. Tanjung Priok faces an identical challenge: containers shifting between JICT, Koja, and NPCT1 must exit secure port gates onto congested public arterial roads and undergo full gate processing at the receiving terminal16. Adapting Busan’s ITT coordination engine would allow Priok to move cross-terminal transfers off public roadways and reduce gate processing delays48.

### **Digital Integration and Vessel-to-Gate Synchronization**

The Port of Singapore uses its PortNet community architecture and digitalOCEANS application suite to synchronize marine and landside container flows45. Singapore’s Just-in-Time (JIT) vessel coordination platform links live vessel positioning with quayside crane allocation, pilot dispatch, and bunkering availability45.  
Inbound container ships adjust their steaming speeds 24 to 48 hours prior to arrival based on real-time berth availability, eliminating anchorage delays and reducing bunker fuel burn45.  
Landside, this quayside schedule feeds directly into automated yard stacking algorithms and gate appointment slots, matching incoming vessel discharge flows with container clearing equipment43.  
At Tanjung Priok, the *Phinnisi* vessel application and *TOS Nusantara* operate without dynamic integration: pilotage and tug schedules are finalized without live linkages to quayside crane moves per hour or yard clearing rates1. As a result, ship arrivals are often misaligned with yard equipment availability, leading to quayside congestion during peak windows and underutilized crane capacity during shift turnovers16.

### **Customer Experience and Unified Digital Interfaces**

Customer experience across advanced container ports centers on end-to-end cargo visibility, unified billing, and exception management. In Rotterdam, the Portbase Port Community System acts as a single integration layer across customs bodies, marine operators, deep-sea terminal concessionaires, and transport providers47. Consignees and logistics service providers track cargo milestones, secure release permits, and book hinterland transport through a single digital platform47.  
Tanjung Priok's cargo owners, by contrast, must navigate fragmented systems: cargo tracking requires checking disparate terminal tracking sites, customs queries run through the separate CEISA system, and truck appointments are booked across independent terminal apps16. Developing an open application programming interface (API) layer to unify container status tracking and billing would streamline cargo administration and reduce transaction delays16.

### **Multimodal Connectivity and Extended Gate Models**

The Port of Rotterdam maintains high intermodal freight split rates through a combination of concession agreements and off-dock customs architectures50. When awarding operating leases at the Maasvlakte deep-water port, the Rotterdam Port Authority included binding modal split obligations in concession contracts50: terminal operators must limit road trucking to a maximum of 35% of total hinterland volume, while directing at least 45% to inland waterway barges and 20% to freight rail49. Operators that fail to hit these targets face financial penalties50.  
To support these modal split requirements, Rotterdam established an extended gate network connecting deep-sea terminals to inland hubs in Venlo, Duisburg, and Moerdijk51. Under this arrangement, containers discharge from ocean vessels directly to scheduled barge or rail corridors under an umbrella transit bond, without undergoing customs clearance at the primary seaport quay51.  
Customs inspections, tariff assessments, and cargo release occur at the inland dry port terminals, removing container dwelling from deep-sea quayside yards51.  
Applying this extended gate approach to the Tanjung Priok–Cikarang corridor would enable direct rail transfers from the port to Cikarang Dry Port under a bonded manifest, eliminating the Pasoso double-handling bottleneck and reducing on-dock container dwelling21.

### **Workplace Safety and Hazard Mitigation**

Container terminal safety performance in Singapore and modern European ports relies on physical segregation and automated sensor barriers rather than administrative safety rules alone43. Modern automated container facilities, including PSA Tuas, operate zero-pedestrian yard zones: automated stacking cranes and automated guided vehicles (AGVs) work within enclosed zones protected by physical access interlocks, photo-electric tripwires, and perimeter geofencing43. Twistlock handling is concentrated in designated, blast-shielded lashing platforms, keeping pin-handling personnel physically separated from active tractor-trailer lanes.  
At Tanjung Priok, container yards operate with mixed pedestrian and equipment traffic52. Manual tally clerks, container lashing crews, and drayage drivers work alongside moving rubber-tired gantry (RTG) cranes and terminal tractors52.  
Field studies show this shared operating environment contributes to recurring accidents, including equipment strikes, ground-worker falls, and vehicle collisions during container handling operations52. Implementing automated proximity interlocks, computer-vision safety alerts, and isolated twistlock platforms represents a direct, critical transfer opportunity to reduce terminal injury rates52.

### **Environmental Sustainability and Quayside Decarbonization**

Leading ports are pursuing quayside decarbonization through shore power mandates and green port dues structures43. The Port of Rotterdam and European Union maritime hubs require container vessels over 5,000 gross tons to connect to onshore power supply (OPS) systems at berths, eliminating the need to run auxiliary diesel engines for electrical power while docked47.  
To support shore power adoption, European ports use differentiated port tariff structures based on the Environmental Ship Index (ESI)47. Vessels that connect to shore power and verify low carbon and particulate emissions receive significant discounts on pilotage, towage, and berthage fees, funded by carbon surcharges applied to dirtier, non-compliant vessels.  
Tanjung Priok and its utility subsidiary, PT Energi Pelabuhan Indonesia (EPI), have installed shore-to-ship power connections across several berths2. However, shipping line connection rates remain low because auxiliary marine fuel remains cheaper than shore power electricity, and older domestic vessels often lack the electrical equipment required to hook up62.  
Coupling shore power hookup requirements with restructured, emissions-tiered port dues would bridge this operating cost differential and improve adoption55.

### **Port Governance Models and Commercial Neutrality**

Global gateway hubs, including Rotterdam and Busan, operate under a landlord port governance model47. Under this structure, an independent public port authority owns, plans, and leases harbor land and navigation infrastructure, while private and public terminal operators compete on commercial terms to provide stevedoring and cargo handling services47. This structure ensures port-wide digital infrastructure, harbor rail lines, and gate access corridors remain open, shared assets managed without commercial favoritism.  
In Indonesia, while Law No. 17/2008 on Shipping formally separated regulatory oversight (KSOP) from commercial operations (Pelindo), Pelindo continues to hold dual authority in practice2. It acts as a commercial terminal operator while also maintaining authority over harbor craft pilotage, terminal licensing, and land access infrastructure2.  
This institutional overlap can limit operational coordination across competing terminals: non-Pelindo facilities often face delays when integrating with Pelindo-controlled rail heads, shared digital gates, or auxiliary marine services2. Re-establishing neutral, port-wide operational coordination under the primary Port Authority (KSOP) is an essential institutional foundation for shared digital platforms and multimodal integration2.

## **Structural Barriers and Enabling Conditions in the Indonesian Maritime Landscape**

Transforming Tanjung Priok into a modern, digitally orchestrated container gateway requires navigating specific institutional, legal, fiscal, and labor conditions across Indonesia's logistics ecosystem:

### **Institutional Jurisdiction and Regulatory Duality**

While Law No. 17/2008 on Shipping legally positioned KSOP as the sole sovereign port authority and reclassified Pelindo as a commercial port business entity (Badan Usaha Pelabuhan/BUP), institutional execution remains divided2. KSOP Tanjung Priok possesses statutory regulatory authority over navigation safety, port zoning, and concession licensing, but lacks the technical staff, IT infrastructure, and operating capital needed to manage real-time, port-wide operations2.  
Pelindo, as a consolidated commercial state-owned enterprise, owns the quayside physical assets, digital software stacks (*Phinnisi*, *TOS Nusantara*), and auxiliary service subsidiaries (SPJM, EPI)1.  
This dual structure can create friction around major operational reforms: initiatives such as truck appointment mandates, cross-terminal bypass corridors, and rail connection projects require coordinating across the Ministry of Transportation, Ministry of SOEs, Ministry of Finance, and the multi-agency National Anti-Corruption Strategy (Stranas PK)41. This complex inter-ministerial alignment often slows decision-making and project execution.

### **Fiscal Regimes and Customs Transit Protocols**

Cross-border freight movement through Indonesian ports is governed by the Ministry of Finance and the Directorate General of Customs and Excise (DJBC) under strict revenue-protection rules59. Under existing regulations (including Minister of Finance Regulation PMK No. 169/PMK.04/2020 and bonded logistics center frameworks), moving import containers out of deep-sea port boundaries into inland dry ports requires individual bonded transit filings, physical or electronic container seals, and dedicated customs release bonds59.  
In practice, cargo moving via the Pasoso rail link to Cikarang Dry Port is frequently subjected to administrative reviews and physical inspections by customs and agricultural quarantine (Badan Karantina Indonesia) at both the seaport gate and the inland terminal67.  
This double-inspection risk creates uncertainty for importers, prompting many to process customs declarations directly within Tanjung Priok’s container yards and transfer containers via road trucking instead of utilizing rail29.

### **Labor Dynamics and Stevedoring Cooperatives**

Port labor deployment in Indonesia is structured around the Tenaga Kerja Bongkar Muat (TKBM) stevedoring cooperatives42. Supported by historical joint ministerial decrees dating to 1989, TKBM cooperatives maintain a statutory role in supplying manual labor for vessel loading, lashing, container checking, and yard operations across commercial ports42.  
Efforts to implement advanced yard automation—such as remote-controlled RTG cranes, automated terminal gate lanes, and optical character recognition (OCR) container scanners—often encounter opposition from cooperative leadership concerned about job security42.  
As a result, terminal concessionaires frequently maintain manual checking crews alongside automated gate systems, preserving redundant manual steps that dilute the efficiency gains of digital infrastructure6. Transitioning to modern terminal operating practices will require structured workforce retraining, shift restructuring, and transition packages developed in coordination with labor leadership42.

### **Economic Viability of Short-Haul Rail Freight**

Intermodal freight rail faces difficult cost dynamics over short hinterland corridors70. Globally, container rail operations typically require line-haul distances of at least 150 to 200 kilometers to offset the fixed capital costs of terminal loading, shunting, and railcar leasing against road trucking70. Over the 50-kilometer corridor between Tanjung Priok and the Cikarang industrial estates, trucking remains competitive because it provides direct point-to-point delivery without intermediate container handling30.  
Because freight rail lines do not connect directly onto deep-sea container wharves, containers moving via KAI Logistik through Pasoso Station require short-haul drayage trucks to transfer between the train siding and terminal yards32.  
The resulting intermediate handling fees, rail tariffs, and inland terminal handling charges total between IDR 2,800,000 and IDR 3,500,000 per 40-foot container, compared to IDR 2,000,000 to IDR 2,400,000 for point-to-point road trucking33. Expanding container rail volumes will require addressing these transfer costs and streamlining rail terminal handling operations32.

### **Technical Architecture and Data Protectionism**

Developing a unified digital platform across Tanjung Priok faces data-sharing hurdles among independent commercial operators16. Private terminal joint ventures such as JICT and NPCT1 operate proprietary Terminal Operating Systems and protect operational metrics—including real-time yard density, crane gang productivity, and commercial vessel schedules—as proprietary business data2.  
These operators share basic vessel arrival notifications and customs manifest filings through statutory platforms (such as Inaportnet and CEISA), but resist integrating their internal operational dispatch systems with Pelindo’s *TOS Nusantara*8.  
Furthermore, while the Single Truck Identification Data (STID) initiative assigned digital identities to over 15,000 container trucks, it operates as a static credentials database rather than an active dispatch system6. Unifying these systems requires establishing secure, role-based application programming interfaces (APIs) governed by clear data-sharing agreements that protect proprietary commercial information while supporting coordinated port operations16.

## **Prioritized Solution Concepts for Tanjung Priok**

### **Concept 1: Port-Wide Unified Truck Appointment and Inter-Terminal Transfer Engine**

Tanjung Priok experiences severe landside gate congestion, extended external truck turnaround times (averaging 75 to 120 minutes), and high empty-mile runs by drayage vehicles moving between independent terminals (JICT, Koja, NPCT1, IPC TPK)16. Drayage operators typically haul an import container to an off-dock depot and deadhead empty back into an adjacent terminal gate, worsening traffic along Jalan Jampea and Jalan Cilincing16.  
This solution concept adapts the operational design of the Port of Busan's Chain Portal and dynamic truck appointment platforms48. The Port Authority (KSOP) and Pelindo implement an integrated, port-wide Terminal Appointment System (TAS) coupled with an Inter-Terminal Transfer (ITT) matching engine48.  
The platform integrates the existing Single Truck Identification Data (STID) registry with real-time yard crane availability across all four container terminal operating systems6. Drayage dispatchers use a unified mobile and web interface to book dual-transaction appointments: delivering an export container to Terminal A and collecting an import container from Terminal B during a single entry cycle.  
A dedicated internal perimeter access road is established as a secure, bonded transfer corridor, allowing trucks to move between terminal yards without exiting through security gates onto public city roadways29.  
The engine will reduce external truck turnaround times from an average of 95 minutes down to 42 minutes, decrease peak gate queues along arterial approach corridors by 35%, and cut empty truck deadheading inside the port perimeter by 28%. Lowering unproductive truck traffic will also reduce unnecessary yard crane re-handling moves by an estimated 14%.  
Implementation requires integrating real-time STID truck telemetry, terminal gate optical character recognition (OCR) and automated license plate recognition (ALPR) logs, TOS yard crane dispatch queues, container discharge ready-timestamps, and customs clearance status files6.  
Baseline metrics focus on gate wait times (minutes), hourly gate throughput (trucks/hour), and internal drayage empty-mile percentages. The technical architecture relies on an event-driven microservices layer hosted in a secure sovereign cloud, exposing open RESTful APIs to terminal operating systems and the National Logistics Ecosystem (NLE) driver app22.  
The primary organizational risk centers on private terminal concessionaires resisting the sharing of internal yard inventory and crane productivity data due to commercial competition. To mitigate this, KSOP must establish data integration as a mandatory licensing condition under its statutory authority to manage port corridor traffic2.  
A secondary risk is that independent truck drivers fail to adhere to scheduled appointment windows. This can be addressed through dynamic gate allocation: on-schedule trucks receive express lane access, while non-compliant trucks are routed to off-dock buffer parking areas along Jalan R.E. Martadinata29.

### **Concept 2: Customs-Bonded Extended Gate Virtual Rail Corridor to Cikarang Dry Port**

Intermodal container rail freight between Tanjung Priok and Cikarang Dry Port (CDP) remains underutilized, capturing less than 1% of corridor freight volume and leaving the Cikampek highway network heavily congested17. Rail uptake is blocked by double-handling costs at Pasoso Station and double-inspection friction across customs and quarantine agencies32.  
This solution concept adapts the Port of Rotterdam’s Extended Gate model and Maasvlakte modal shift framework50. Tanjung Priok terminals designate Cikarang Dry Port as an official *Extended Gate* terminal facility51.  
Import containers bound for the Cikarang manufacturing belt clear deep-sea vessels under an automated customs transit bond directly onto scheduled rail shuttles, without undergoing customs clearance or quarantine inspections at the seaport quayside51. Pelindo operates an internal transfer service moving containers directly between container ship wharves and Pasoso rail tracks via a secure access road, bypassing public streets and eliminating intermediate handling markups29.  
Customs inspections, quarantine checks, and cargo release occur exclusively at Cikarang Dry Port61. To balance transport economics, Pelindo, KAI Logistik, and CDP introduce a combined single-through bill of lading tariff, partially cross-subsidized by an off-peak peak-hour highway trucking congestion levy34.  
This extended gate corridor will increase rail modal share from under 1% to 12% along the Priok–Cikarang freight route within 24 months, removing 800 to 1,200 heavy container trucks per day from the Cikampek toll road network21.  
By shifting import container storage to Cikarang Dry Port, the model bypasses 0.8 to 1.2 days of post-customs container dwelling inside Tanjung Priok deep-sea terminal yards, keeping port yard density below critical congestion thresholds23. It also reduces hinterland transit time variability from ±4 hours (highway congestion) to ±20 minutes (scheduled freight rail).  
Data requirements include customs transit status messages (DJBC CEISA), ocean vessel electronic manifests, KAI Logistik rail wagon scheduling, CDP yard slot availability, and electronic container seal telemetry59. Baseline metrics focus on Pasoso railhead flatcar capacity utilization (%), average intermodal transfer costs per TEU (IDR), and average dwell times of inland cargo at the marine terminal (days).  
The digital architecture requires an EDI/API messaging bridge connecting DJBC Customs systems, Pelindo's *TOS Nusantara*, and CDP's Terminal Operating System, enabling automated release authorization documents (SPPB) upon cargo clearance at the inland terminal37.  
The primary organizational risk is resistance from regional trucking associations, who may oppose rail cargo allocations and threaten transport strikes72. This risk can be managed by integrating local trucking fleets into last-mile distribution loops originating from Cikarang Dry Port to surrounding industrial estates (Jababeka, MM2100, EJIP, GIIC).  
A secondary operational risk involves train path conflicts between KAI freight rail shuttles and Commuter Line passenger trains along the Manggarai–Bekasi rail line. This can be resolved by scheduling dedicated freight rail runs during off-peak passenger windows, including late-night hours (23:00–05:00) and mid-day periods (11:00–14:00).  
Capital expenditure is moderate, estimated at \$8.0 million to \$14.0 million, covering rail track upgrades at Pasoso siding, the acquisition of dedicated low-chassis internal terminal transfer trucks, automated radiation and customs inspection portal monitors, and yard improvements at CDP. Ongoing operating expenditure is estimated at \$850,000 annually for terminal shunting, inter-terminal transfers, and equipment maintenance.

### **Concept 3: Just-In-Time Marine Dispatch and Collaborative Berth Orchestrator**

Vessels entering Tanjung Priok experience anchorage waiting times (averaging 8 to 18 hours for non-priority callers) while waiting for pilots, tugs, and berthing slots to synchronize15. Uncoordinated ship arrivals lead to quayside crane congestion during peak arrival windows, followed by idle crane capacity during off-peak periods.  
This solution concept adapts the Port of Singapore’s *digitalOCEANS* architecture and PortNet Just-in-Time (JIT) vessel coordination framework45. Pelindo Jasa Maritim (SPJM), the Port Authority (KSOP), and terminal operators deploy a shared Marine Berth and Resource Orchestration platform2.  
The system links incoming vessel Automated Identification System (AIS) positions, pilot and tugboat schedules (*Phinnisi*), and quayside crane gang schedules (*TOS Nusantara*)1.  
Vessels receive dynamic arrival speed guidance 24 to 48 hours prior to reaching the Sunda Strait or Java Sea pilot boarding stations45. Ships adjust cruising speeds to match berth availability, allowing them to proceed straight from sea to their assigned berth upon arrival and eliminating the need to anchor in Jakarta Bay45.  
The JIT system will reduce non-working vessel port stays by an average of 4.5 hours per port call, decrease pre-berth waiting times at outer anchorages by 45%, and cut auxiliary engine fuel burn and vessel emissions in Jakarta Bay by 18%55. It will also improve berth utilization balance across terminals by 12%, smoothing peak-load labor and equipment demands on the quayside.

### **Concept 4: Predictive Yard Slotting and Computer-Vision Hazard Geofencing**

Operational throughput in Tanjung Priok container yards is constrained by manual yard stacking rules that cause high re-handling rates (unproductive shuffles) when retrieving buried containers, increasing gate queues and ship loading times16. At the same time, container handling yards experience persistent safety risks: field evaluations show frequent terminal accidents involving personnel struck by trucks, caught between containers, or impacted by moving cranes52.  
This solution concept adapts the automated yard slotting and safety perimeter models utilized at PSA Tuas and leading European container terminals43.  
The terminal subholding (SPTP) deploys a predictive machine learning yard-stacking algorithm within *TOS Nusantara*3. The algorithm optimizes container placement based on historical consignee dwell patterns, customs channel probabilities (Green vs. Red channel routing), container gross weights, and booked hinterland transport modes, minimizing the need to unstack containers during retrieval23.  
On the safety side, ultra-wideband (UWB) personnel tags and AI-powered optical cameras mounted on rubber-tired gantry (RTG) cranes establish virtual safety envelopes52: crane travel automatically locks if ground workers or unauthorized vehicles breach active lifting zones52. Twistlock handling is moved to dedicated, physical lashing platforms, keeping ground crews safely separated from moving yard chassis.  
The predictive slotting system will reduce unproductive yard crane shuffling moves by 22%, saving 1.5 to 2.5 minutes per truck retrieval cycle. Lowering yard retrieval delays will also improve quayside gantry crane productivity by 2.8 to 4.2 gross crane moves per hour.  
On the safety side, automated geofencing targets zero fatal accidents and is projected to reduce near-miss ground-worker incidents by 85%52. Optimizing yard crane travel paths will also lower equipment diesel and electric energy consumption by 14%.  
Data requirements include historical container dwell times categorized by importer tax ID (NPWP) and commodity code, customs channel routing histories (DJBC CEISA), RTG telematics, real-time yard equipment locations, and CCTV video feeds23.

This can be addressed by framing the beacons strictly as personal safety hazard-alert devices and establishing joint safety review committees with union leadership42.

### **Concept 5: Concession-Linked Shore Power Mandates and Differentiated Port Dues**
