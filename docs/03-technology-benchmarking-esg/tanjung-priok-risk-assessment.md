# **Comprehensive Risk, Governance, and Resilience Assessment of Tanjung Priok Port**

## **Port Profile, Operational Architecture, and Administrative Governance**

Tanjung Priok Port (UN/LOCODE: IDTPP) operates as the primary commercial maritime gateway of Indonesia, handling over 50 percent of the nation's containerized cargo and serving as the primary logistics link for the industrial manufacturing belts of West Java, notably Bekasi, Cikarang, and Karawang1. Annual container throughput at the complex has stabilized around 7.6 million Twenty-foot Equivalent Units (TEUs), with overall volume trending toward 8.3 million TEUs across roughly 14,000 commercial vessel calls1.  
The quayside and container yard architecture is divided among major international joint ventures, dedicated commercial operators, and state-owned multipurpose handling entities.

Dedicated freight rail infrastructure exists within the terminal complex, but intermodal rail absorbs less than 3 percent of the port’s aggregate container throughput1. This creates a severe structural reliance on road transport that leaves the port vulnerable to external highway bottlenecks7.

## **Statutory Frameworks and International Maritime Compliance**

Port operations at Tanjung Priok are governed by a complex matrix of Indonesian statutes and mandatory international conventions that regulate maritime commerce, facility security, dangerous cargo handling, and environmental protection.  
The domestic statutory framework is anchored by Law No. 17 of 2008 on Shipping (*UU No. 17/2008 tentang Pelayaran*), which restructured the Indonesian port sector by formally separating sovereign regulatory responsibilities from commercial port operations22. Under this law, landlord authority and administrative enforcement were reassigned to the Port Authority (*Otoritas Pelabuhan*) and Harbormaster (*Syahbandar*), while Pelindo was repositioned as a licensed terminal operating company (*Badan Usaha Pelabuhan* \- BUP)22.  
Environmental and maritime protection mandates derive from Government Regulation No. 21 of 2010 on Maritime Environmental Protection (*PP No. 21/2010*), which requires port operators and berthed vessels to establish waste management systems and contingency arrangements against shipboard pollution24. This is reinforced by Minister of Transportation Regulation No. PM 51 of 2015, which directs terminal operators to provide certified Port Reception Facilities (PRF) capable of processing vessel sludge, oily bilge water, sewage, and garbage without causing operational delays37.  
Handling procedures for hazardous cargo are regulated by Minister of Transportation Regulation No. PM 16 of 2021 on the Procedures for Handling and Transporting Dangerous Goods at Ports25. This statute sets requirements for advance Dangerous Goods Declarations, spatial segregation in container yards, certified handling personnel, and direct delivery mandates for high-hazard explosive and radioactive classes39.  
To reduce landside congestion, administrative regulations—including Ministry of Transportation Decree KM 60 of 2019 and circulars from the Port Authority—mandate the Single Truck Identification Data (STID) framework and the Terminal Booking System (TBS) to control and verify all commercial trucks entering terminal gates40.  
At the international level, compliance centers on the International Ship and Port Facility Security (ISPS) Code, incorporated into domestic law under Ministry of Transportation Decree KM 33 of 2003 and Regulation PM 134 of 201643. All deep-sea container terminals at Tanjung Priok (JICT, TPK Koja, and NPCT1) operate as ISPS-compliant facilities holding valid Statements of Compliance of a Port Facility (SoCPF)43. These facilities maintain designated Port Facility Security Officers (PFSOs), restricted quayside zones, biometric and RFID access controls, optical container scanning, and three-tiered Maritime Security (MARSEC) readiness procedures27.  
Compliance with the International Maritime Dangerous Goods (IMDG) Code is mandatory under SOLAS Chapter VII and MARPOL Annex III, governing the labeling, packaging, and segregation of chemical shipments39. Finally, SOLAS Chapter VI Regulation 2 requires terminals to implement Verified Gross Mass (VGM) checks, utilizing certified weighbridges at gate-in points to prevent misdeclared container weights from destabilizing vessel stowage plans10.

## **Systemic Operational, Industrial, and Environmental Risk Analysis**

### **Quayside Operations and Industrial Safety**

The combination of rapid vessel turnaround targets, heavy machinery operations, and dense traffic flows generates persistent industrial safety hazards across quayside and yard environments45. Primary physical risks include dropped container loads, wire rope and rigging failures, gantry collisions, and vehicles striking ground personnel or terminal infrastructure32.  
A fatal incident in January 2024 underscored these dangers when the structural boom of a mobile truck crane collapsed directly onto the cabin of a container truck at the port, fatally crushing the resting driver32. Forensic inquiries pointed to mechanical fatigue, inadequate equipment inspection, and a failure to enforce clear safety exclusion zones around active lifting equipment32.  
Driver fatigue and microsleep incidents remain recurring factors in vehicle collisions across the port access roads and terminal aprons, driven by long external wait times and around-the-clock operations32. Additionally, collisions between heavy commercial container haulers and smaller vehicles or foot traffic at critical terminal bottlenecks (such as the Jalan Industri and Jalan RE Martadinata intersections) have repeatedly caused severe fatalities16.  
High-pressure operations also increase the risk of container stack collapses. Uneven weight distribution from improperly declared cargo, combined with wind shear during seasonal squalls, can compromise stack integrity. Reach stackers, empty handlers, and rubber-tyred gantry cranes operate in close proximity to terminal prime movers, where blind spots and driver fatigue during night shifts can lead to serious industrial accidents32.

### **Dangerous Goods Stacking and Hazardous Material Propagation**

Tanjung Priok handles large volumes of dangerous goods (DG) classified under IMDG Classes 1 through 9, including industrial chemicals, compressed gases, and petrochemical feedstocks39. These materials present severe fire, explosion, and toxic exposure risks if storage rules are breached2.  
An operational incident occurred in late 2021 when a container holding hazardous chemical cargo caught fire at the NPCT1 facility, triggering emergency response procedures by terminal teams, the harbormaster, and local fire services to isolate the container and prevent flames from spreading to nearby stacks34. A broader incident in the surrounding port logistics district involved a major fire that engulfed 51 ISO tank containers carrying oils and solvents, illustrating the risk of rapid fire spread in high-density container yards32.  
Under local port circulars and international IMDG requirements, storage protocols are tied to hazard classification44. IMDG Class 1 (explosives) and Class 7 (radioactive cargo) cannot be stored in standard terminal container yards; they must be cleared via direct loading to or from transport vehicles (*truck loosing*)44. For dangerous cargo in Classes 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, and 9, storage in designated yard blocks is legally capped between 24 and 96 hours44.  
However, inspections periodically find issues with incomplete placarding, inaccurate cargo manifests, and improper vertical and horizontal segregation of incompatible chemicals39. When inland transport links fail or customs documentation is delayed, dangerous goods containers can remain in terminal stacks beyond permitted limits, creating concentrated blast and toxic vapor hazards next to main berths23.

### **Digital Interdependence, TOS Vulnerabilities, and Cyber Disruption**

Terminal operations at Tanjung Priok rely heavily on automated and interconnected digital platforms. Facilities operate advanced Terminal Operating Systems (TOS)—including Navis N4, TOS Nusantara, and ECON+—which interface with quayside crane controls, optical character recognition gates, RTG auto-steering networks, and central billing systems9.  
The systemic vulnerability of this setup was demonstrated in November 2022 when a major TOS database and network disruption paralyzed JICT52. The outage disabled automated gate operations, container tracking, and equipment allocation, halting all container handling52. Within hours, trucks backed up across main access routes and into the wider North Jakarta road network, stranding export shipments and imposing severe demurrage costs on logistics providers before manual and failover systems restored partial operations52.  
External software dependencies introduce additional points of failure. The customs clearance processing system—the Customs-Excise Information System and Automation (CEISA)—operated by the Directorate General of Customs and Excise, has suffered multi-day technical outages (most notably in July 2021 and August 2023\)30. Because containers cannot legally exit terminal premises without electronic Customs Release Notes (*Surat Persetujuan Pengeluaran Barang* \- SPPB), customs server downtime paralyzed outward movements23. Yard Occupancy Rates (YOR) climbed above 80 percent, filling container yards, slowing vessel discharge operations, and forcing authorities to clear backlogs using manual paper processing23.  
Risks to digital infrastructure are further underscored by wider cyber events in Indonesia, such as the June 2024 Brain Cipher ransomware attack on the National Data Center (*Pusat Data Nasional Sementara 2* \- PDNS-2)58. That incident disabled digital immigration checks, disrupted customs processing, and delayed commercial vessel clearances across major transport hubs, prompting terminal operators to migrate mission-critical operational data away from public infrastructure into secure, segregated private networks59.

### **Geotechnical Subsidence, Hydrometeorological Extremes, and Tidal Inundation**

Tanjung Priok is exposed to chronic geotechnical and hydrometeorological hazards typical of the North Jakarta coastal plain. Extensive groundwater extraction, coupled with heavy surface infrastructure and natural compaction of alluvial soils, drives significant land subsidence across the harbor district61. Geodetic and satellite studies show subsidence rates in Kelurahan Tanjung Priok and surrounding logistics zones ranging from 3.0 to 10.0 centimeters per year64.  
Ongoing ground subsidence lowers surface elevations relative to mean sea level, increasing the port's vulnerability to tidal inundation (*banjir rob*)61. During astronomical spring tides and monsoonal storm surges, seawater regularly overtops seawalls and floods access roads, off-dock container yards, and gate complexes67.  
While modern facilities like NPCT1 were constructed on elevated reclaimed platforms (+2.5 to \+3.0 meters above mean sea level), older operational zones, off-dock storage areas, and perimeter roads sit at much lower elevations10. Long-term flood protection depends heavily on the National Capital Integrated Coastal Development (NCICD) master plan, which involves constructing offshore revetments and landward coastal dikes63. However, project delivery timelines have experienced recurring delays, with final completion targets for critical protective dikes pushed back to 2029–203069.  
As a result, terminal operations rely on localized polder drainage systems, retention basins, and stationary pumping stations operated by the DKI Jakarta Water Resources Agency (*Dinas SDA*) and Pelindo67. During the northwest monsoon (November to March), extreme downpours often coincide with high tides, overwhelming drainage channels and flooding critical transit corridors like Jalan Cakung-Cilincing and Jalan Jampea18. This cuts off terminal gates and creates traffic gridlock across the logistics network even when quaysides remain dry18.

### **Drayage Fragmentation, Yard Dwell Times, and Supply-Chain Congestion**

Container logistics at Tanjung Priok operate in an inelastic, road-dependent transport environment. When vessel arrival surges coincide with system outages or post-holiday freight peaks, the road network can quickly seize up74. In mid-April 2025, a sudden post-Eid logistics surge, coupled with delayed vessel schedules, triggered massive gridlock that brought traffic around Tanjung Priok to a complete halt74. Truck backups extended for tens of kilometers across regional tollways, leaving drivers stranded for multiple days and inflicting an estimated Rp 100 billion in direct losses on trucking companies74. Similar chain-reaction gridlocks occurred in early 2026 when traffic accidents along primary freight arterials completely blocked access to the terminal gates18.  
This congestion stems from several deep structural imbalances within the port logistics ecosystem:

* An estimated 51 percent of container trucks entering TPK Koja and neighboring facilities travel empty, reflecting trade imbalances and fragmented depot networks that generate unproductive truck trips across the metropolitan road network7.  
* Data from trucking associations indicates that over 90 percent of regional container trucks are aging diesel vehicles prone to mechanical breakdowns on port access flyovers, where a single immobilized vehicle can block access to terminal gates18.  
* While the Terminal Booking System (TBS) and Single Truck Identification Data (STID) were implemented to smooth truck arrivals across eight-hour booking slots, slot allocations frequently fail to match real-time container releases, customs inspections, and off-dock depot operating hours40. As a result, trucks queue along public roads rather than arriving in controlled, predictable waves20.

## **Emergency Management Architecture, Mutual Aid, and Business Continuity**

Emergency response planning at Tanjung Priok is structured across marine pollution control, industrial firefighting, and digital business continuity. Each area is coordinated among specialized government agencies, commercial operators, and neighboring industrial facilities.  
Marine oil spill response is governed by Presidential Regulation No. 109 of 2006 (*Perpres 109/2006*), which establishes a three-tiered operational framework78. The Head of KSOP Utama Tanjung Priok serves as the designated Mission Coordinator (Incident Commander) for Tier 1 spills occurring within port water boundaries (*DLKr* and *DLKp*)22. Pelindo's marine service arm, alongside commercial terminal operators, maintains first-response pollution equipment: rapidly deployable offshore and harbor oil booms, heavy-duty brush and weir skimmers, floating temporary storage bladders, and approved chemical dispersants38.  
Terminal contingency plans are supported through mutual aid agreements with state oil and gas companies, including Pertamina Hulu Energi Offshore North West Java (PHE ONWJ) and Pertamina Patra Niaga, which can deploy offshore support vessels, heavy oil-skimming systems, and specialized cleanup personnel during larger Tier 2 coastal emergencies in Jakarta Bay83. If a spill exceeds local containment and threatens wider international shipping lanes in the Java Sea, operational command escalates to the Tier 3 National Oil Spill Response Team under the Directorate General of Sea Transportation, which can mobilize specialized international strike teams from Oil Spill Response Limited (OSRL) in Singapore78.  
Industrial firefighting and quayside fire suppression integrate terminal safety units with the North Jakarta Municipal Fire and Rescue Sub-Agency (*Suku Dinas Damkar Jakarta Utara*)88. On the water, firefighting capability centers on Pelindo's tugboat fleet, operated by PT Jasa Armada Indonesia Tbk28. These tugs feature certified FiFi-1 systems equipped with dual high-capacity water monitors and aqueous film-forming foam (AFFF) cannons capable of attacking shipboard fires, bunker platform infernos, or quayside stack blazes from the seaward approaches28. On land, deep-sea berths maintain pressurized fire main loops, booster stations, and regularly spaced hydrants, backed by mobile foam trailers and dry chemical units designed to suppress chemical and fuel fires in dangerous goods storage yards44.  
Business continuity practices are designed to keep cargo moving during major infrastructure outages, aligning with ISO 22301 Business Continuity Management Systems (BCMS)84. In the event of primary database or telecommunications failures, terminal operating plans establish maximum acceptable downtime thresholds (such as JICT's operational target of restoring services within 21 hours)52.  
IT resilience relies on warm and hot offsite Disaster Recovery Centers (DRCs) backed by redundant dark fiber lines and private microwave connections19. If the central TOS platform suffers an extended outage, terminal operations shift to emergency manual operating procedures: gates switch to paper-based inspection sheets, handheld tally devices, and manual optical character logging, while RTGs and quay cranes transition to local voice communication over isolated VHF channels29.  
Similarly, when central customs systems (CEISA) go offline, the Tanjung Priok Customs Office activates emergency manual protocols, issuing physical gate passes under strict security oversight to keep cargo moving and prevent yard occupancy from reaching gridlock levels29. Quayside power reliability is maintained through dedicated dual-feed connections from the national power grid (PLN), supported by on-site diesel generator banks and industrial uninterruptible power supply (UPS) units that sustain STS cranes, reefer container racks, and server rooms during power outages90.

## **Strategic Recommendations and Resilience Engineering Roadmap**

### **Intermodal Drayage Rationalization and Gate Fluidity**

The high frequency of landside traffic paralysis requires restructuring how container trucks are scheduled, processed, and held outside terminal gates. The Port Authority, terminal operators, and haulage associations (such as Aptrindo) need to coordinate the Terminal Booking System (TBS) with real-time operations across the logistics chain41. Rather than treating terminal gate slots independently, TBS booking allocations should dynamically sync with shipping line delivery orders, customs release notifications (SPPB), and the working hours of external empty container depots23. Introducing automated slot re-allocations when vessel discharge times shift will prevent drivers from arriving at the gates before their cargo is ready41.  
To keep truck queues off public roads, port stakeholders should develop off-dock staging parks equipped with automated pre-gate clearance kiosks91. Drayage trucks traveling along the Cakung-Cilincing or ATP corridors can be held in these off-dock yards, completing STID identification, container seal checks, and weighbridge verification before proceeding to the terminals19.  
At the same time, regional logistics authorities should encourage empty container depots to transition to 24-hour operations matching the main terminals75. Expanding off-peak gate operations and standardizing depot shifts will reduce empty-truck trips, flatten daytime traffic peaks, and prevent trucks from parking along public access corridors overnight7.

### **OT/IT Cyber Resilience and Offline Fallback Capacity**

Given the systemic disruptions caused by past software and data outages, terminal operators need to strengthen both their digital defenses and their offline continuity capabilities. Facilities should adopt a zero-trust network architecture that strictly isolates corporate IT systems from quayside Operational Technology (OT) networks96. Industrial firewalls and unidirectional data diodes should be installed to protect SCADA networks, quay crane programmable logic controllers (PLCs), and yard equipment positioning systems from cyber threats originating on office computers or public networks59.  
To mitigate recurring disruptions from external platforms like the national customs system (CEISA), terminals should deploy local edge-processing systems at all gate complexes52. These edge nodes can cache valid customs and booking tokens locally, allowing gates to continue processing pre-cleared trucks during central network outages without backing traffic out into public streets29.  
Furthermore, terminal operators should maintain active-active cloud disaster recovery architectures with independent data centers outside the capital district59. Regularly conducting live failover drills will ensure that backup databases and TOS services can be brought online well within established recovery time objectives, such as JICT’s 21-hour restoration target52.

### **Industrial Heavy Lifting and Quayside Risk Governance**

Preventing industrial accidents during heavy lifting operations requires combining strict safety rules with automated safety technology. Terminal operators should deploy artificial-intelligence-assisted proximity detection systems and computer-vision blind-spot cameras across all yard equipment, including rubber-tyred gantry cranes, reach stackers, and heavy forklifts45. These systems automatically warn operators and bring machinery to a controlled stop if ground personnel or unauthorized vehicles enter dangerous operating zones45.  
Clear safety rules must also be enforced to protect drayage drivers during container loading and unloading32. Terminals should maintain designated, protected waiting shelters and enforce policies requiring drivers to exit their cabs and remain inside safety enclosures during container handling, preventing injuries in the event of an equipment failure or dropped load32.  
Additionally, KSOP and terminal safety managers should establish rigorous non-destructive testing (NDT) programs for all lifting equipment45. Regular ultrasonic and magnetic-particle inspections of crane boom structures, hoist drums, wire ropes, and twistlock mechanisms will identify metal fatigue early, preventing catastrophic failures like the January 2024 mobile crane collapse32.

### **Hydrological Protection and Subsidence Adaptation**

Addressing the double challenge of land subsidence and sea-level rise requires both localized engineering adjustments and active coordination with regional flood control projects. Because North Jakarta continues to sink between 3.0 and 10.0 centimeters annually, terminal engineering teams should avoid laying large, rigid concrete slabs across newly built or renovated container yards64.  
Instead, yards should utilize flexible, high-capacity interlocking concrete block paving laid over engineered sub-bases8. Interlocking pavers accommodate differential ground settlement without cracking, can be regraded quickly during routine maintenance, and prevent equipment damage or container stack instability caused by uneven ground66.  
At the drainage level, terminal polder networks should be upgraded with automated sluice gates and backup diesel-driven stormwater pumps67. Installing dedicated generator power for all polder pumps ensures drainage systems continue operating even if local power fails during severe monsoon storms67.  
At the regional level, Pelindo and the Port Authority should coordinate closely with the Ministry of Public Works and Housing (PUPR) and the DKI Jakarta Provincial Government to align internal seawall construction with the wider National Capital Integrated Coastal Development (NCICD) coastal defense plan63. Raising outer seawalls and building planned coastal revetments will shield the port’s low-lying access corridors and perimeter logistics areas from severe tidal inundation (*banjir rob*) through 2030 and beyond63.

### **Hazardous Cargo Safeguards and Environmental Reception Infrastructure**

Managing dangerous cargo requires tighter administrative screening combined with dedicated containment infrastructure. Terminal operators should integrate automated validation checks into their digital booking platforms to verify dangerous goods declarations against IMDG segregation matrices before containers arrive at the gate39. The software should automatically block incompatible chemical containers from being assigned to adjacent yard slots, removing the risk of dangerous combinations in storage stacks44.  
In the yard, hazardous materials blocks should feature specialized impermeable epoxy pavements, secondary containment bunds, dedicated drainage sumps, and fixed chemical dry-powder or foam fire-suppression systems44. To enforce dwell-time limits, port regulators should establish automated tracking rules that levy escalating demurrage fees on containers exceeding the mandatory 24- to 96-hour storage windows, with clear escalation paths to transfer abandoned cargo to secure, isolated handling depots outside the primary terminal23.  
To protect the marine environment, the Port Authority and terminal operators should modernize Port Reception Facilities (PRF) to simplify vessel waste disposal under MARPOL Annexes I, IV, and V37. Incorporating standard waste disposal fees into regular vessel berthing tariffs, rather than charging separately for every disposal service, removes the economic incentive for ships to bypass official facilities, encouraging proper disposal of oily bilge water and sludge and reducing illegal dumping in Jakarta Bay38.  
Finally, KSOP and terminal concessionaires should conduct joint, unannounced Tier 1 oil spill response exercises alongside municipal emergency units and offshore oil and gas operators79. Regular drills ensure boom deployment teams, skimmer crews, and incident commanders can rapidly contain and clean up bunker spills before oil spreads into active navigation channels79.
