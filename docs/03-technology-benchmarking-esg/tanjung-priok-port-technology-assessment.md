# **Digital Port Infrastructure, Operational Systems, and Cross-Ecosystem Integration at Pelindo and the Port of Tanjung Priok**

The Port of Tanjung Priok serves as Indonesia's primary maritime gateway, handling approximately half of the nation's containerized international and inter-island commerce1. Following the 2021 enterprise merger of state port operators Pelindo I through IV into PT Pelabuhan Indonesia (Persero), the harbor transitioned from fragmented legacy administration to an integrated operational model2. Tanjung Priok's terminal ecosystem spans wholly owned operating entities such as IPC Terminal Petikemas (IPC TPK) and PT Pelabuhan Tanjung Priok (PTP Nonpetikemas), major operating joint ventures including the Jakarta International Container Terminal (JICT) and TPK Koja, and deep-water developments such as New Priok Container Terminal One (NPCT1)4. Coordinating marine fairway traffic, quayside container cranes, yard blocks, automated road gates, and dry port hinterlands across these operating environments requires complex software suites, sensor arrays, and multi-agency regulatory interfaces2.

## **Marine Digital Platforms and Fairway Traffic Management**

Vessel coordination at Tanjung Priok operates through a multi-tiered regulatory and operational interface bridging the Ministry of Transportation’s Inaportnet platform with Pelindo’s proprietary vessel operating system, Phinnisi, engineered by PT Integrasi Logistik Cipta Solusi (ILCS / Pelindo Solusi Digital)2.  
Inaportnet functions as the sovereign government-to-business (G2B) digital clearinghouse administered by the Directorate General of Sea Transportation (Ditjen Hubla) and the local Port Authority (Kantor Kesyahbandaran dan Otoritas Pelabuhan / KSOP)2. It serves as the legal gateway through which commercial shipping lines and domestic shipping agents submit statutory documentation, including pre-arrival vessel notifications, dangerous goods manifests, and applications for entry and departure clearances (*clearance-in* and *clearance-out*)2. By consolidating inter-agency verifications into a single submission architecture, Inaportnet replaced paper manifests, cutting administrative vessel turnaround overhead significantly2.  
Once statutory clearance is confirmed within Inaportnet, operational management transitions directly to Phinnisi via automated application programming interfaces (APIs)12. Phinnisi functions as Pelindo's marine enterprise resource planning and dispatch system, executing an end-to-end "order-to-cash" workflow12. The platform structures the physical servicing of the vessel through discrete stages:

* Validating vessel arrival notices (*Pemberitahuan Kedatangan Kapal* / PKK) against declared ship particulars12;  
* Processing service requests (*Permintaan Pelayanan Kapal dan Barang* / PPKB) for pilotage, tug assistance, and berthing quays12;  
* Conducting financial security checks and automated load balancing against available pilot personnel and harbor tug assets12;  
* Issuing digital work orders (*Surat Perintah Kerja* / SPK) directly to shiphandling teams12;  
* Recording the operational logs signed by the ship master (*master's receipt*) to generate downstream invoices (*pranota* and *nota*)12.

Physical execution along the water approaches depends on direct telemetry feeds from the local Vessel Traffic Services (VTS) and the Port Operations Command Center (POCC)2. When an arriving vessel passes the outer approaches of the Tanjung Priok Traffic Separation Scheme (TSS), its telemetry is picked up by shore-based Automatic Identification System (AIS) transponders, coastal radar installations, and optical surveillance arrays2. VTS operators monitor fairway safety, while Phinnisi reconciles real-time positioning data against confirmed operational service bookings12.  
This platform integration enables dynamic pilot and tug deployment decisions12. In traditional harbor operations, pilots and tugboats were scheduled using static paper queues, leading to vessels idling at outer anchorages while waiting for pilot boarding2. Within Phinnisi, incoming vessel parameters—such as length overall (LOA), beam, maximum dynamic draft, deadweight tonnage, and engine power—are algorithmically matched against marine pilot certifications and tug engine bollard-pull ratings12.  
When weather variations or fairway congestion alter vessel transit velocities, the system updates ship progress alerts across the POCC and recalibrates dispatch schedules2. Digital dispatch notifications are routed to pilot tablets, and harbor tug crews receive work orders synchronized with estimated vessel arrivals at the breakwater12. This minimizes idle vessel wait times at outer anchorages and reduces auxiliary tug fuel burn2.

## **Terminal Operating Systems and Quayside/Yard Automation**

The terminal landscape across Tanjung Priok comprises a mixture of commercial enterprise software platforms and internal applications developed by Pelindo7. Following corporate consolidation, Pelindo introduced two core operational solutions developed by ILCS: TOS Nusantara (PTOS-C) for container terminals and PTOS-M for multipurpose terminals, designed to standardize processes across disparate operating entities11.

### **Container Terminal Operations**

Dedicated container facilities—including IPC TPK, JICT, TPK Koja, and NPCT1—rely on high-performance TOS architectures to manage ship, yard, and gate workflows7. At IPC TPK, TOS Nusantara (PTOS-C) orchestrates vessel planning, crane assignments, yard management, and billing in a unified platform6. The planning suite receives electronic data interchange (EDI) transmissions directly from container shipping lines, interpreting EDIFACT BAPLIE bay plans and COPRAR loading and discharge instructions25.  
The system analyzes vessel stability parameters, hydrostatic curves, and weight distributions to generate crane split programs for Super Post-Panamax Quay Cranes along the berth26. In parallel, automated sequencing routines route internal terminal vehicles (ITVs) between quay cranes and designated yard blocks, minimizing vehicle congestion under the crane portals24.  
At JICT, operations have historically relied on CyberLogitec’s OPUS platform, supplemented by bespoke operational modules and selected Navis N4 applications6. Equipment tracking across the quay and yard interfaces with programmable logic controllers (PLCs) on ship-to-shore (STS) cranes and rubber-tyred gantry cranes (RTGs), capturing spreader lock/unlock events, container identification numbers, and hoisting speeds in real time26.  
TPK Koja utilizes an integrated OPUS platform alongside components of TOS Nusantara, relying on differential GPS (DGPS) sensor arrays and terminal truck telemetry to manage yard moves and eliminate lost containers24. At NPCT1, terminal planning is linked to electrified rubber-tyred gantry cranes (e-RTGs) and automated optical character recognition (OCR) gate portals, using optimization algorithms to allocate yard storage locations based on container dwell times and customs clearance states9.

### **Multipurpose Terminal Operations**

Non-containerized cargo, including break-bulk, dry bulk, liquid bulk, and heavy project machinery handled by PTP Nonpetikemas, is managed via PTOS-M5. Because non-containerized cargo lacks standard dimensional footprints and handling characteristics, PTOS-M approaches planning through spatial zoning and cargo property categorizations21.  
The system configures gang handling rosters and allocates harbor mobile cranes based on expected discharge speeds (metric tons per hour)21. For project cargo and steel coils, PTOS-M cross-references ground bearing capacities against declared cargo weights to identify safe open-storage locations21. For bulk commodities, it coordinates direct discharge operations (*langsam*), synchronizing ship cranes with arriving fleets of dump trucks to bypass open-yard staging and avoid unnecessary ground handling21.

To minimize unproductive container shifts (*reshuffling*)—where buried import containers must be moved to retrieve a target unit—TOS software analyzes container departure predictions, transport modes, and customs clearance profiles8.  
At the quayside, the systems deploy dual-cycling algorithms: instead of an internal terminal truck running empty after depositing an export container at the quay crane, the TOS assigns it a discharged import container for transport back to a neighboring yard block8. This approach reduces empty truck trips, cuts diesel fuel burn, and maintains gross crane moves per hour (GMPH) across working berths2.

## **Landside Interoperability, Gate Automation, and Hinterland Corridors**

The interface between terminal yards and the urban road network represents a major operational chokepoint across Tanjung Priok2. To manage external container truck traffic, Pelindo and the Port Authority deployed the Single Truck Identification Data (STID) framework alongside Automated Gate Systems (AGS) and the Terminal and Truck Booking System (TTBS/TBS)2.  
Historically, individual terminal operators issued proprietary entry cards, requiring drayage drivers to carry multiple credentials and complete manual paperwork at each gate36. The STID system replaced this fragmented approach with a centralized, port-wide database administered by the Port Authority36. Every commercial prime mover operating within Tanjung Priok must be inspected and registered in the system1. The STID profile links the truck's vehicle identification number and physical RFID tag to corporate ownership records, vehicle safety inspection certificates (*KIR*), and registered driver credentials within the Driver Identification Data (DID) platform1. Over 23,000 trucks operate under active STID registration, establishing a verified baseline for automated landside access control41.  
At the physical terminal boundaries, Automated Gate Systems (AGS) process arriving drayage vehicles through a synchronized, automated screening sequence:

> 1. When a prime mover enters the gate approach lane, high-resolution optical character recognition (OCR) gantries capture the container's structural ISO code, size dimensions, and door orientation, while License Plate Recognition (LPR) cameras record the vehicle's registration plates9.  
> 2. Concurrently, high-frequency RFID interrogators read the driver's STID card, and smart weighbridges or weigh-in-motion scales capture gross axle weights to determine verified payload weights in compliance with SOLAS gross mass regulations36.  
> 3. The AGS processing gateway cross-checks these physical sensor readings against the TOS booking index and customs clearance records39. If all data points match, the system raises the boom barrier and generates a digital Chassis Movement Slip (CMS) sent directly to the driver's mobile device or an automated receipt kiosk36.  
> 4. The CMS directs the driver to a designated yard block, row, tier, and slot, while notifying the corresponding RTG gantry of the incoming pickup or drop-off24.  
> 5. If an anomaly occurs—such as an unregistered tractor unit, an unreadable ISO container code, an expired safety inspection certificate, or an active customs hold—the system halts the transaction, illuminates warning signals, and diverts the truck into an adjacent exception lane to prevent gate line blockages36.

Complementing gate automation, the Terminal and Truck Booking System (TTBS/TBS) smooths traffic flows across surrounding access roads20. Historically, freight forwarders and drayage operators dispatched trucks during concentrated evening windows, generating major gridlock along the Jakarta port highway network, including Jalan Pelabuhan Raya, Jalan Jampea, and the Cakung-Cilincing corridor38.  
The TBS addresses this by requiring logistics operators to reserve specific 60- to 120-minute arrival slots prior to dispatching equipment1. The TOS dynamically calculates available gate slots based on real-time yard crane deployment and quay vessel discharge intensity24. If a specific yard block experiences high RTG utilization or is handling heavy vessel discharge volumes, the TBS restricts new reservations for that zone and shifts booking availability to off-peak hours38.  
Hinterland cargo connectivity is further extended via dedicated links to inland intermodal dry ports, most notably Cikarang Dry Port (CDP), located within the industrial manufacturing corridors of Bekasi and Karawang44. Operating under its assigned international port code (IDJBK), CDP functions as a dry port extension of Tanjung Priok16. Enterprise Electronic Data Interchange (EDI) feeds synchronize cargo statuses between marine terminal TOS platforms and CDP management software16.  
Through this pipeline, containers marked for Cikarang bypass storage staging at Tanjung Priok16. Bonded shuttle services operated by PT Kereta Api Logistik (KAI Logistik) and bonded highway haulers transport containers inland under unified customs transit documentation (BC 1.2)16. This moves import declarations, quarantine inspections, and empty container returns away from the marine terminal yards, freeing up quayside storage capacity and lowering overall port dwell times (*dwell time*)44.

## **Stakeholder Integration: Customs, Port Authorities, and Logistics Networks**

Terminal efficiency at Tanjung Priok depends on data exchange with regulatory agencies and external trade stakeholders2. The primary integration connects terminal operating systems with the CEISA 4.0 ecosystem (Customs-Excise Information System and Automation), administered by the Directorate General of Customs and Excise (Ditjen Bea dan Cukai / DJBC) under the Ministry of Finance33.  
Operational data sharing runs through the TPS Online 2.0 system (*Tempat Penimbunan Sementara Online*), which establishes continuous API connections between temporary storage yards and CEISA 4.033. This automated interface supports several core customs workflows:

* Before vessel arrival, ocean carriers submit import cargo declarations via INSW to CEISA 4.0, which generates the inward manifest (*Manifes Kedatangan Sarana Pengangkut* / BC 1.1)16. This manifest data passes down to the TOS via TPS Online, linking arriving containers to legal customs records33.  
* As containers are offloaded from vessels and scanned into terminal stacks, the TOS transmits real-time arrival logs to CEISA 4.0, confirming customs custody without manual intervention33.  
* Following import duty settlement, risk profiling, and document verification, CEISA 4.0 issues an electronic Release Permit (*Surat Persetujuan Pengeluaran Barang* / SPPB)33. The SPPB token automatically syncs with the TOS, updating the container's status from "Customs Hold" to "Release Cleared" and authorizing the Automated Gate System to admit retrieving trucks33.  
* For outbound cargo, electronic export declarations (*Pemberitahuan Ekspor Barang* / PEB) sync with gate arrival confirmations, verifying physical arrival before vessels receive loading authorizations33.

These interactions are further coordinated through the National Logistics Ecosystem (NLE) and the INSW portal10. The NLE links government-to-government (G2G) clearances with commercial business-to-business (B2B) supply chains33. A clear example of this integration is the Single Submission (SSm) Quarantine-Customs platform16.  
Historically, agricultural and biological imports required separate documentation, individual handling requests, and redundant unsealing operations from both customs and quarantine inspectors52. Under SSm Quarantine-Customs, the importer submits a single electronic declaration through INSW, allowing both authorities to evaluate risk models simultaneously16.  
If a physical inspection is required, the system schedules a joint inspection window at the terminal inspection yard (*behandle*)29. The TOS automatically routes the target container to the inspection facility, where quarantine specialists and customs officers review the cargo concurrently29. This single-stop process has eliminated redundant container moves and reduced handling costs for regulated agricultural and cold-chain cargo52.

## **Command Centers, Telemetry Streams, and Operational Tactical Control**

Port operations at Tanjung Priok are coordinated through the Port Operations Command Center (POCC) and terminal Planning and Control Towers3. The POCC brings maritime operations, terminal berths, security monitoring, and hinterland logistics under a shared operating picture, serving as a unified control room for the entire harbor basin3.  
The POCC continuously ingests and correlates telemetry streams from across the port:

* Maritime surveillance systems combine shore radar sweeps with terrestrial AIS transponder data, monitoring commercial vessel coordinates, transit speeds, and heading vectors through the fairway channels2. Hydrographic stations measure dynamic tidal curves and basin depths, alerting dispatchers to available draft clearances12.  
* Terminal operations stream telemetry from programmable logic controllers (PLCs) installed on ship-to-shore (STS) cranes and electrified rubber-tyred gantry cranes (e-RTGs)27. This data tracks real-time crane moves per hour, spreader hoist motor loads, wind speeds from crane anemometers, and container position changes24.  
* Terminal vehicle tracking utilizes differential GPS (DGPS) receivers mounted on internal terminal trucks, mapping vehicle positions and wait times against dynamic TOS work queues10.  
* Landside monitoring systems integrate optical camera feeds with induction-loop vehicle sensors to evaluate traffic congestion along port gates, staging lanes, and external access expressways10.

This real-time telemetry allows the command center to identify operational disruptions early and coordinate tactical responses across systems3. When an inbound vessel is delayed by adverse weather in the Sunda Strait, POCC dispatchers identify the revised arrival time through AIS tracking feeds12. Marine controllers adjust pilot assignments and tugboat deployments in Phinnisi, reassigning open service windows to vessels waiting at inner anchorages2.  
Simultaneously, the terminal control tower updates the TOS with the new Estimated Time of Arrival (ETA), adjusting berth lineups and reassigning quay crane gangs to working vessels2. This prevents cranes from sitting idle and keeps longshore labor productive3.  
When container yard densities approach high-congestion thresholds—typically above 75% to 80% yard utilization—the command center adjusts operating rules across the terminal network38. Dispatchers can divert empty container returns to off-dock depots (*depot peti kemas*), reallocate transfer lanes, or adjust gate booking allowances within the TBS to protect ongoing vessel discharge programs38.  
If an emergency incident occurs, such as a dangerous goods container alert or an industrial equipment fire, command center dispatchers isolate the affected yard block within the TOS, dispatch emergency teams, and adjust terminal traffic routing to maintain safe operations across surrounding berths32.

## **Interoperability Bottlenecks, Data Hygiene Defects, and Operational Fragility**

Despite ongoing modernization, digital operations across Tanjung Priok face integration frictions, technical debt, and data discrepancies that create operational bottlenecks3.  
A primary operational challenge stems from software fragmentation across terminals7. Because Tanjung Priok operates through a mixture of wholly owned subsidiaries, joint ventures, and international consortia, software standardization across terminals remains incomplete7. IPC TPK runs TOS Nusantara, JICT utilizes legacy OPUS systems with bespoke internal software, and NPCT1 operates specialized commercial TOS platforms9.  
These systems use varying business logic, container slotting rules, API data formats, and electronic gate interfaces7. When cargo moves between terminals (such as inter-terminal transfers for domestic feeder transshipment), data exchanges frequently require manual reconciliation or intermediate data batching, creating information blind spots and documentation delays2.  
A second vulnerability involves operational dependencies on external government platforms, particularly Inaportnet and CEISA 4.015. While TPS Online 2.0 processes data smoothly during standard operations, disruptions or network latency within central customs databases can stall port processes33. If electronic release permits (SPPB) fail to transmit from customs databases to terminal TOS systems, terminal gates cannot release import containers33.  
Because automated gates are configured to reject uncleared containers, customs messaging delays trigger immediate truck queues at terminal gates34. Within two hours, backups can spill out onto arterial highways like the Tanjung Priok Access Toll Road26. During extended outages, terminals must activate manual paper-based contingency protocols, requiring physical stamps and paper customs declarations26. This manual processing reduces gate processing speeds by more than 80%, disrupting landside traffic flows10.  
Data hygiene issues also create operational drag within the ecosystem:

* In the STID database, thousands of transport units operate under temporary "conditional approvals" because of outdated vehicle fitness certifications (*KIR*) or discrepancies in fleet ownership records40. When physical sensor errors occur at the gate—such as unreadable RFID tags or faded tractor license plates—the Automated Gate System rejects the vehicle, forcing it into manual exception lanes and creating gate line congestion36.  
* Carrier-provided BAPLIE and COPRAR stowage EDI files frequently show data discrepancies, including misstated container tare weights, conflicting dangerous goods (IMDG) hazard classes, and missing port-of-discharge (POD) codes25. If these manifest discrepancies are not caught before vessel arrival, the TOS must halt operations mid-discharge to re-index containers, disrupting quay crane operations and lowering crane moves per hour24.  
* While the Truck Booking System aims to smooth traffic flows, booking compliance among drayage operators remains inconsistent38. Trucks frequently arrive outside their assigned booking windows or enter gates without prior reservations38. Gate operators face the choice of rejecting these vehicles (which worsens local traffic congestion outside port gates) or accepting them manually, which disrupts optimized yard stacking plans8.

## **Cybersecurity Posture, Operational Technology Risks, and Personal Data Governance**

As port infrastructure becomes increasingly automated and interconnected, its vulnerability to cyber threats expands57. As a designated national Critical Information Infrastructure (CII), operational disruptions at the Port of Tanjung Priok can disrupt domestic supply chains, delay industrial manufacturing inputs, and generate broad economic impacts59.  
The convergence of enterprise IT platforms with physical Operational Technology (OT) represents a critical structural vulnerability57. Modern container cranes, e-RTGs, power substations, and automated gate barriers rely on industrial control systems (ICS) and SCADA networks managed by programmable logic controllers (PLCs)27. In older equipment, these systems rely on legacy communication protocols—such as Modbus TCP or unencrypted industrial Ethernet—that lack built-in authentication, cryptographic verification, or network segmentation57.  
When remote equipment monitoring tools, TOS telemetry agents, and vendor maintenance links connect to these industrial systems, they create potential network pathways for malicious actors to bridge enterprise networks and access physical port machinery57. A compromised PLC network could allow unauthorized actors to manipulate crane limit switches, disrupt container spreader interlocks, or disable automated gate boom barriers, turning a software exploit into a physical safety hazard57.  
Ransomware remains an acute operational threat57. Modern container terminals operate tight, just-in-time logistics schedules where unexpected TOS downtime can halt quayside and gate operations, stranding thousands of containers within hours26. Threat actors targeting regional critical infrastructure deploy multi-stage attacks: gaining network access through corporate phishing or third-party supply chain vulnerabilities, moving laterally across logistics networks, and encrypting critical databases—including container location tables, equipment dispatch queues, and customs gate clearances57. Restoring these systems from backups requires manual container audits across vast yard facilities, which can impact port operations for days10.  
Data privacy and data sovereignty present additional compliance challenges under Indonesia's regulatory framework, specifically Law No. 27/2022 on Personal Data Protection (UU PDP)59. The STID and Driver Identification Data (DID) platforms process personal identifiable information (PII) on tens of thousands of commercial drivers, including national identification numbers (NIK), biometric facial profiles, driving license details, employment relationships, and real-time movement histories across port facilities36.  
Managing this information across multiple commercial platforms (including Pelindo servers, terminal joint ventures, and trucking association portals) increases the risk of data leakage40. Under UU PDP requirements, port platforms must enforce strict role-based access controls, data encryption for stored and transmitted records, clear data retention limits, and formal data processing consent agreements57.  
To address these vulnerabilities, the National Cyber and Crypto Agency (BSSN) oversees port cybersecurity standards and incident response protocols59. BSSN sets mandatory security baselines for maritime critical infrastructure, requiring port operators to establish dedicated Computer Security Incident Response Teams (CSIRT), enforce regular vulnerability assessments, and deploy continuous security operations centers (SOC)57. These operational safeguards are essential to protect the integrity of automated trade facilities that handle critical national supply chains2.

A major analytical application is predictive vessel arrival and dynamic berth scheduling12. Currently, vessel Estimated Times of Arrival (ETA) rely heavily on static declarations submitted by shipping lines12. Machine learning models combining satellite AIS tracking, historical voyage data across the Malacca and Sunda straits, weather forecasts, and harbor basin congestion metrics can predict actual arrival times with high precision2.  
These predictive arrival timelines feed directly into automated berth allocation engines12. Using mixed-integer linear programming (MILP) and reinforcement learning, the TOS can dynamically reassign quay berths, minimizing total vessel waiting times and optimizing crane deployments across working vessels2.  
A second high-impact opportunity lies in dynamic yard optimization and automated container slotting8. Container terminal productivity is frequently constrained by unproductive container shifts (*reshuffling*) during collection operations8. Machine learning classification models can predict dwell times (*dwell time prediction*) for inbound import containers before they are unloaded45. By analyzing historical importer collection behaviors, commodity codes, customs declaration channels (green lane versus red lane inspection probability), and cargo delivery profiles, the system can assign a predictive collection index to each container33.  
Containers with a high probability of rapid collection can be positioned on top tiers, while containers predicted to stay longer are stacked in lower rows8. Deep reinforcement learning agents can continuously optimize yard block layouts, scheduling automated re-marshalling moves during quiet night shifts to prepare stacks for expected morning collection appointments8.  
Predictive maintenance across critical terminal equipment offers substantial efficiency gains26. STS cranes, e-RTGs, and mobile harbor cranes operate under demanding industrial cycles where unplanned equipment breakdowns mid-vessel can paralyze berth operations26. Equipment control systems can capture vibration spectra, motor current draws, gearbox temperatures, and spreader cycle counts, streaming this telemetry to central analytical models24.  
Machine learning anomaly detection models can identify component degradation patterns weeks before mechanical failure occurs, enabling maintenance teams to schedule repairs during planned vessel gaps and avoid disruptive operational outages26.  
Computer vision systems can similarly enhance automated gate throughput and terminal safety9. Beyond standard optical character recognition (OCR), edge-computing camera arrays can analyze video streams to perform automated physical damage inspections, detect container structural defects, identify missing or damaged customs bolt seals, and automatically recognize hazardous cargo placards (IMDG hazard classes)9.  
At the same time, computer vision cameras installed in yard blocks can detect personnel entering crane operating zones, automatically triggering equipment safety stops to protect terminal ground crews13.  
Landside traffic corridors can also benefit from dynamic traffic management2. Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) analyzing GPS data from STID-registered trucks, municipal traffic cameras, and port access toll sensors can predict incoming gate arrivals across 30-, 60-, and 120-minute planning horizons36.  
The Terminal and Truck Booking System (TBS) can use these predictions to dynamically throttle booking slots38. If congestion builds along external access highways, the system can dynamically reduce gate quotas, hold non-critical empty container returns in off-dock buffer zones, and open dedicated express lanes for time-critical export containers, stabilizing traffic flows throughout the logistics corridor38.

### **Dimension Analysis**

Vessel Operations and Fairway Control achieves a Level 3.8 rating because the deployment of Phinnisi across the consolidated Pelindo network has integrated previously manual dispatch logs into a structured digital workflow12. Operational data flows from Inaportnet down through pilot work orders, reducing vessel waiting times for pilot boarding and anchoring overhead2.  
However, closing the gap to Level 5 will require transitioning from human-directed dispatch queues to automated berth planning engines2. These systems must calculate tidal windows, channel draft thresholds, and harbor basin turning circle availability in real time, orchestrating vessel line-ups with minimal manual replanning2.  
Terminal Operations and Quayside Logistics rates at Level 3.6, reflecting the deployment of modern container operating systems, automated crane PLCs, and e-RTG fleets across primary container terminals24. Despite these technical assets, operational fragmentation between wholly owned Pelindo terminals and operating joint ventures limits ecosystem-wide efficiency7.  
Yard slotting remains governed by static business rules rather than dynamic machine learning models8. Achieving Level 5 autonomy requires deploying reinforcement learning algorithms to optimize container stacking configurations in real time, dynamically reallocating yard slots based on predictive dwell times to minimize costly container reshuffling8.  
Landside Access and Intermodal Gate performance stands at Level 3.336. The STID master registry and Automated Gate Systems have significantly reduced average gate processing times, while the Terminal and Truck Booking System (TBS) provides a structure for managing peak traffic demand10.  
Nevertheless, landside flow remains vulnerable to external traffic congestion on access toll roads, driver non-compliance with scheduled booking windows, and incomplete STID data hygiene38. Advancing to Level 5 requires deploying dynamic slot quotas linked to real-time highway telematics, paired with computer vision systems capable of performing automated structural damage and seal inspections at gate portals9.  
The Regulatory Agency and Customs Ecosystem exhibits the highest maturity at Level 4.1, driven by the rollout of CEISA 4.0, TPS Online 2.0, and the National Logistics Ecosystem10. The automation of customs release permits (SPPB) and the integration of joint customs-quarantine inspections have replaced paper-based workflows with unified digital pipelines33.  
The primary barrier to reaching Level 5 is system resilience: the terminal gate architecture depends on central customs databases, meaning external network slowdowns can halt gate operations33. Progressing to full autonomy requires implementing decentralized edge-computing nodes capable of securely caching clearance tokens and processing gate transactions during network outages26.  
Data Intelligence and Cyber Resilience is the least mature dimension, rating at Level 2.73. While the Port Operations Command Center provides valuable situational monitoring, operational analytics remain largely retrospective, focusing on post-shift productivity reports rather than predictive intelligence3.  
Crucially, cybersecurity controls across the interface between enterprise IT and physical operational technology (OT) require substantial reinforcement57. Securing this environment requires implementing zero-trust network segmentation, continuous anomaly detection on SCADA networks, and strict data governance policies to protect driver personally identifiable information under Law No. 27/2022 (UU PDP)57.

## **Operational Control and IT Management Audit Framework**

The following ten technical audit questions are designed for operational-control reviews with Pelindo’s engineering leadership, terminal directors, and systems integration specialists:

> 1. In the event of latency, database locking, or unplanned outages within CEISA 4.0 or TPS Online 2.0, what edge caching and offline token validation mechanisms decouple terminal gate admissions from live external API calls to prevent drayage queues from spilling onto the surrounding highway network26?  
> 2. How do integration layers between IPC TPK (running TOS Nusantara/PTOS-C), JICT (running OPUS), and NPCT1 standardize and reconcile conflicting data formats, container ISO attributes, and equipment telematics during inter-terminal transfers without requiring intermediate batching or manual intervention7?  
> 3. To what extent do current TOS algorithms incorporate predictive machine learning—such as analyzing historical consignee collection profiles, customs inspection channels, and forwarder behaviors—to dynamically optimize import container stacks and reduce unproductive container shifts8?  
> 4. How does the Terminal and Truck Booking System (TBS) adjust booking quotas in real time based on active yard crane availability, and what automated gate mechanisms handle trucks that arrive outside their scheduled booking windows without creating road gridlock38?  
> 5. What automated data validation processes identify and resolve expired roadworthiness certifications (*KIR*), conflicting fleet ownership records, and unverified temporary approvals within the STID registry before vehicles reach automated gate lanes36?  
> 6. How does the Phinnisi platform integrate satellite and terrestrial AIS position data, channel tidal variations, and traffic separation congestion metrics into pilotage dispatch systems to automatically optimize pilot assignments and reduce vessel waiting times2?  
> 7. What specific network segmentation standards (such as ISA/IEC 62443 zones and conduits) protect supervisory control and PLC networks on ship-to-shore cranes and e-RTGs from vulnerabilities originating in enterprise billing, web-based TOS portals, or third-party maintenance links57?  
> 8. When live telemetry in the Port Operations Command Center detects severe operational disruptions—such as crane productivity falling below target thresholds, gate queues exceeding physical limits, or yard density surpassing 80%—what automated workflows alert operational controllers and redirect resources3?  
> 9. How do port platforms manage the storage, encryption, and access controls for driver identification data (including biometric records and national identity numbers) across private terminal systems and trucking associations in accordance with Law No. 27/2022 on Personal Data Protection36?  
> 10. What telemetry metrics (such as hoist motor vibration spectra, PLC error trends, and inverter heat profiles) stream continuously from quayside container cranes into predictive maintenance models to identify equipment failures before they disrupt active vessel operations24?

## **Strategic Conclusions**

The digital transformation across Pelindo’s operations at the Port of Tanjung Priok demonstrates meaningful progress, evolving from disconnected legacy platforms toward a coordinated port management ecosystem2. Implementing standardized platforms such as Phinnisi for vessel management, TOS Nusantara and PTOS-M for terminal operations, Single Truck Identification Data for gate access, and TPS Online 2.0 for customs coordination has reduced vessel turnaround times, accelerated gate transactions, and improved operational transparency2.  
However, realizing the full potential of these digital investments requires addressing persistent operational frictions3. Software and architectural fragmentation across operating joint ventures, system vulnerabilities tied to external customs databases, and data hygiene challenges in drayage registries continue to create operational bottlenecks7. Furthermore, the port's analytical capabilities rely predominantly on historical reporting rather than predictive machine learning3.  
To advance along the digital port maturity spectrum, Pelindo must focus on core strategic priorities:

* Deploying predictive vessel arrival and dynamic berth optimization algorithms within Phinnisi and the POCC to optimize water-side asset utilization12.  
* Implementing machine-learning yard slotting engines within TOS environments to systematically reduce unproductive container shifts8.  
* Enhancing system resilience at terminal gates through decoupled local processing nodes to maintain fluid operations during external network slowdowns26.  
* Strengthening operational technology cybersecurity across crane, gate, and SCADA infrastructure in coordination with BSSN standards to protect critical maritime infrastructure57.

By closing these integration, analytical, and security gaps, the Port of Tanjung Priok can transition from a digitally connected port to an intelligent, self-optimizing maritime logistics hub capable of supporting Indonesia's growing role in international trade2.
